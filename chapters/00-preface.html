Preface

A book of principles alone is not much use on a Tuesday morning when a product team wants to ship something in three weeks and you have to decide what to ask them. Knowing that AI should be fair, transparent, accountable, and safe does not by itself tell you what question to ask first, what an inventory record needs to contain, or what to do when a vendor declines to answer the question that matters most.

This book is about the method. It assumes you already accept that AI should be governed and moves directly to the harder question of how. What do you ask, in what order, and what should exist by the time you are done asking? What does an inventory record contain, and what breaks when a field is missing? How do you test a system for a property that cannot be directly specified? What do you do when a vendor declines to answer the question that matters most?

Three commitments shape it.

The first is that governance is a practice with a method rather than a set of positions. Identify who is affected and what they need. Turn obligations and needs into requirements specific enough to test. Trace those requirements through to evidence. Then check whether any of it worked. That sequence runs through every chapter, applied to systems in the middle of the book and to the governance function itself at the end.

The second is that predictive, generative, and agentic systems now routinely appear together inside the same deployment. Governance frameworks were built for predictive systems that an organization trained on its own data, and many readers of this book will not train anything themselves. They will select a model somebody else built, inherit that developer’s policies wholesale, and deploy it inside a system that also generates content or takes actions. This book connects governance principles to operating decisions across all three system types, including systems built on externally supplied models, with predictive, generative, and agentic material appearing throughout rather than quarantined into late chapters, and the difference between building and buying running through every stage.

The third is that this subject changes underneath you. Between the first draft of this book and its release, the European Union deferred its high-risk obligations by more than a year because the technical standards that make compliance cheap to demonstrate were not finished. Organizations that had treated the original deadline as immovable had over-invested. Organizations that treated the proposed delay as settled before it was law had stopped work. Both errors came from the same mistake, which was treating a regulatory date as a plan. Where this book states a date, it states when it was checked, and it teaches the practice of tracking change rather than the contents of a calendar.

A note on what this book does not do. Where practice has no good answer, it says so. The chapter on risk assessment includes a section on consulting people who cannot be reached, because the people most affected by a hiring algorithm never interact with the organization deploying it and cannot be interviewed. The available substitutes are partial and the book presents them as partial. An acknowledged gap is more useful than a false resolution, particularly to a student who will meet the gap in practice and needs to recognize it.

This book supersedes Governing AI: Leading Responsible Innovation, which remains available and citable at DOI 10.5281/zenodo.18407349. It is substantially longer and restructured. This edition’s manuscript was last revised in September 2026. Its account of law and standards generally reflects an August 2026 cutoff, except where a specific passage states a later check date. Any date this book states is meant to carry its own check date rather than borrow the manuscript’s.

How to use this book

The book is built to be read front to back and used as reference afterward.

Part I establishes what AI governance is, what law and standards require, and how to find and classify what your organization already operates. Part II follows a system through its life, from the decision to build or buy through to incident response. Part III concentrates the controls that content generation and autonomous action require. Part IV covers the governance function itself. Part V covers building that function and keeping it current.

Each chapter opens with a problem rather than a summary, and states what you should be able to do by the end. Each closes with seven review questions in a fixed pattern. Two ask about comprehension, two are applied, one asks you to find the defects in a described situation, one asks you to choose between two defensible options, and one follows a running case. The last two matter most. Governance work is mostly choosing between imperfect options and explaining the choice.

Figures are meant to be read rather than glanced at, and the aim throughout is to set each one up before it appears and walk through it after. Where a specific figure’s surrounding text falls short of that aim, that is a defect in this book to fix rather than a different design choice.

Three hypothetical organizations recur throughout, which is a more useful device than a fresh example per chapter because it lets consequences accumulate. A decision made badly early produces an incident several chapters later.

The running cases

MEDASSIST · A clinical deterioration prediction tool at Northfield Health, a regional network with one academic medical center and six community hospitals. It scores admitted patients hourly for sepsis risk and alerts nursing staff. Built internally on ten years of the academic center’s records. It carries data representativeness, ground truth that is delayed or absent, alert fatigue, and what human oversight means when the human has eleven other patients. Later it acquires a generative documentation assistant.

FAIRLEND · A consumer credit model at Meridian Financial, a mid-size bank operating in the United States and the European Union. It sets both approval and pricing for personal loans. Built internally, replacing a model that an existing model risk function had governed for a decade. It carries the incompatibility of fairness definitions, proxy discrimination, explanation obligations, and what happens when a new governance function meets an entrenched one.

TALENTSCREEN · A resume screening tool licensed by Calloway Industries, a manufacturer of 14,000 employees hiring across the United States and Europe. The vendor also supplies automated scheduling and candidate communication. Purchased, with no visibility into training data. It carries vendor due diligence, liability that does not transfer with the purchase, transparency to applicants who cannot tell they were screened, and scope creep. Later its scheduling component starts taking actions on its own.

All three are hypothetical. Real cases are drawn from documented sources and identified as such.
