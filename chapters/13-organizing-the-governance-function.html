13. Organizing the Governance Function

Consider a constructed scenario that continues the running FAIRLEND case. Meridian Financial’s board approved a charter for its AI governance function, the document stating that function’s job and powers. On paper, the function existed from the day the board approved the charter.

The charter told the function to review AI systems for fairness, transparency, and risk before deployment. The function reported to the chief risk officer, the executive in charge of managing the company’s risks. Its responsibilities covered every class of AI system, from predictive credit models to generative and agentic tools. Predictive models forecast outcomes, such as whether a borrower will repay. Generative tools create new text or images. Agentic tools take actions on their own.

The charter left one question open. The new function took eighteen months to work out the answer in practice. The question was what happened when the function’s mandate overlapped with a model risk management function. That older function checks that the models a company relies on are sound. It had governed FAIRLEND’s underlying credit models for a decade. It had its own validation standards, which are its rules for how a model must be independently checked. It had its own reporting line. It also had its own settled view of what “model governance” meant, that is, of how models should be overseen. It formed that view before anyone at Meridian used the words “AI governance” at all.

A charter alone does not answer the structural questions. The first question is which operating model an organization chooses, meaning how it divides governance work and authority among its teams. The choice plays out across dozens of individual decision points, not once on an org chart.

The second question is which roles and lines of defense a governance program needs. Lines of defense are layers of responsibility for managing risk. A program needs them to function in practice and not only exist. The third question is what happens when a new AI governance function meets an incumbent function that already has real authority. An incumbent is a function that was in place first.

Structure decides whether a program has opinions or authority. At Meridian, that difference decided whether the function could stop a deployment or could only write a memo about a deployment that went ahead anyway.

What you will be able to do

  • Compare centralized, federated, and hybrid operating models against organizational context and identify where the choice recurs across dozens of decisions instead of only once
  • Apply the three lines model to AI governance, naming which functions occupy which line
  • Design an acceptable use policy that combines a sanctioned alternative, technical controls, and monitoring instead of relying on a written prohibition alone
  • Work through structural conflict with an incumbent function by locating where each function’s mandate starts and stops

13.1 Operating models

Centralized, federated, and hybrid arrangements are design patterns. They are not a performance ranking. Which one suits an organization depends on many factors. These include the decision at issue, applicable law, organizational scale, risk concentration, and local expertise. They also include independence requirements, the rules that keep reviewers separate from the teams they review, and the ability to aggregate evidence from across the organization. Current U.S. bank supervisory guidance on model risk management, the advice issued by the agencies that oversee banks, names these factors explicitly. The guidance says the factors vary by an organization’s risk profile, the mix of risks it faces, and by its size, complexity, and use. The guidance does not settle on one preferred structure.

A centralized model puts governance authority in a single function. That function reviews and approves AI systems across the whole organization. This produces consistent standards and a single point of aggregate visibility, a view of the whole organization. The cost is a bottleneck when the organization deploys AI faster than one central team can review it. A central function can also lack the specific business context that each deploying unit has.

A federated model spreads governance authority to the business units that deploy AI. Each unit applies governance standards within its own domain. This produces responsiveness and decisions that fit local context. The cost is the set of failure modes that section 13.4 develops in detail. Standards drift apart across units. Nobody holds the aggregate picture that regulators and boards need.

A hybrid model splits authority along some other dimension. Section 13.3 develops one candidate split. Like the other two models, that split must be tested against an organization’s own conditions. It must not be assumed to fit.

Which model fits depends on three things. The first is organizational scale. The second is how mature the existing risk functions already are. The third is how fast AI deployment is happening. A small organization that deploys a handful of systems can centralize without creating a bottleneck. A large organization with dozens of business units deploying AI independently will choke a central function that tries to review everything itself.

A hybrid model is not a single fixed arrangement. The word names a general strategy. That strategy splits authority along some dimension other than “everything centralized” or “everything local.” An organization that chooses a hybrid still has to decide which dimension does the splitting.

Some organizations split by risk tier. A risk tier is a level on a scale that sorts systems by how much risk they carry. These organizations centralize review of the highest-risk systems. Local teams self-certify lower-risk ones under a published standard. To self-certify is to confirm for yourself that a system meets the standard.

Some organizations split by system class. They hold predictive systems under an established credit-risk governance function. They route generative and agentic systems through a dedicated review path. These systems are newer and less matched to any existing process.

One such split appears in detail in section 13.3. Comparative evidence has not established that it is superior to every other split an organization might choose for its own conditions.

13.2 The operating model as a recurring variable

The operating model choice is not a single decision that an organization makes once and records on an org chart. It recurs at every governance decision point, independently. An organization that has not noticed this will find it has made the choice differently in different places. It never decided to do so.

Consider five questions. Does the system inventory live centrally, or does each business unit maintain its own? Does a central function classify a system’s risk tier by one standard, or do local teams apply their own assessment? Does deployment go through a central gate, or through local sign-off? Who holds model selection authority? Who owns the vendor relationship that a purchased system requires?

An organization can legitimately be federated on some of these decisions and centralized on others. The pattern must be a deliberate design. It must not be an accident of which team happened to build which process first.

Meridian’s own arrangement illustrates the accident version. Over a decade, its model risk management function had centralized model validation as a matter of course. Meanwhile, each business line had built its own approval process for use cases, the specific tasks an AI system is put to. The business lines did not coordinate with one another. The result was an organization centralized on one axis, model validation, and federated on another, use-case approval. Neither arrangement was chosen on purpose.

13.3 One candidate split: federated at the system layer, centralized at the model layer

One candidate hybrid split divides authority by layer. It does not divide authority by function or business unit. The split follows from the two-record inventory of Chapter 3, an inventory that keeps two kinds of record for the AI an organization uses.

Under this split, local teams own their own deployments. A deployment here means the use case, the affected population, the deployment context, and the day-to-day operation. Chapter 8 through Chapter 10 describe these.

The organization holds model selection, developer terms, and provenance centrally. Developer terms are the contract terms set by a model’s developer. Provenance is the record of where a model came from. Holding these centrally means answering three questions in one place. Which underlying models has the organization vetted? What obligations attach to each supply chain entry point from Chapter 6, each point where an outside model or service enters the organization? What is the model itself, before any local team decides to build a use case on top of it?

Stated this way, the split can look cleaner than it is in practice. Several of the decisions it assigns are normally cross-layer processes, needing work from both layers, and not exclusive territory. Section 13.7 returns to this point in its discussion of governance bodies.

This split deserves consideration, but it does not settle the allocation on its own. It tracks where a defect originates. It also tracks where a defect needs to be caught once and not rediscovered independently. This holds for some decisions at least.

Take a flaw in a purchased model’s training data, the data the model learned from. Or take an undisclosed model update, a change the vendor made to the model without telling its customers. Either problem is substantially a model-layer problem. It will recur in every local deployment built on that model. Catching it centrally, once, can spread the fix everywhere at once.

Now take a mismatch between a system’s intended use case and its actual deployment population. That is substantially a system-layer problem. It is specific to one local team’s context. A central function that reviews every local deployment decision is less well placed here than the team that understands its own population.

Even so, an inventory record, a risk classification, a deployment approval, and a vendor relationship each still need both local input and central coordination in practice. A central inventory needs local data plus central aggregation. A vendor’s obligations can attach differently depending on customization, integration, data, and the specific regulatory role a given deployment plays. The layer split is therefore a starting frame for a decision-rights design, a plan for who may decide what. It is not the design itself.

The binary version of this split, the either-or version, has one layer or the other hold each decision exclusively. Most of these decisions are cross-layer processes instead. They have a proposing party, an evidence-providing party, a challenging party, and an approving party. They do not have a single owner.

Table 13.1 therefore states the split as a decision-rights matrix, which shows who plays which role in each decision, and not as a two-column allocation. Both sources describe layered responsibility instead of exclusive territory for each of these decisions.

13.4 How federated governance fails

Federated governance fails in three predictable ways. A program that has not been warned about them tends to discover all three at once. The first is classification drift. Two business units facing comparable systems classify them at different risk tiers. Without a shared calibration mechanism, a shared way to compare and align decisions across units, federated decisions can drift further apart over time. They do not come back together on their own.

The drift itself is not automatically a defect. A hiring screen and a credit screen can be built on the same underlying model architecture, meaning the same basic design of model. They can legitimately land in different risk tiers. Their intended uses, affected populations, and consequences of error differ. A program that flags every difference as inconsistency would punish sound decisions that fit each unit’s own context. It would not catch a real failure.

The failure to catch is narrower. Two units may face the same use, the same population, and the same stakes. They can still reach different classifications because the reviewers weighed identical evidence differently. Neither reviewer knows the other’s classification exists. No recorded rationale lets either one compare their reasoning with the other’s.

Calibration tells the two cases apart. It means sampling classifications across units. It means comparing the recorded rationale and not only the tier assigned. It also means updating shared guidance where the comparison reveals unwarranted variation, meaning differences with no good reason behind them, and not a defensible contextual difference, one that context justifies. In both cases, the drift stays invisible until that comparison happens or an audit or incident forces it.

The second failure is capture, where a reviewer comes to serve the interests of the group it is meant to check. Local governance is captured by the business it serves. A reviewer embedded in the same business unit whose deployment they are reviewing faces continuous pressure to approve. They share the unit’s incentives and its relationships. Independence erodes gradually, not through any single compromised decision. The reviewer does not experience this as compromise. They experience it as sound decisions made under time pressure by someone who understands the business. That is why capture is hard to detect from inside the arrangement that produces it.

The third failure is lost aggregate visibility. Nobody holds a view of the whole organization. This failure is serious. At least some regulatory reporting obligations attach to the deploying organization, or to a specific provider or deployer role under it. They do not attach to a business unit in isolation.

Under the EU AI Act, Article 26 places monitoring, suspension, and notification duties on the deployer. Article 73 places serious-incident reporting duties on the provider, duties to report serious harmful events. Either duty holder needs a view across whatever business units its own systems span in order to meet the duty. A federated program with no central aggregation point cannot answer that kind of organization-wide or role-wide question. This is true even when every individual business unit could answer its own piece correctly.

An organization should be able to say how many AI systems it operates and how that scope was defined. That is the standard set by the inventory obligation in Chapter 3. Specific sector regulation or supervisory guidance can separately impose that same answer as its own legal recordkeeping duty. An organization has no answer at all if its inventory lives in a dozen disconnected spreadsheets instead of a single record. This holds however well each business unit tracks its own piece.

13.5 Roles and responsibilities

A governance decision needs four explicit answers. Who is responsible for doing the work? Who is accountable for the outcome? Who must be consulted before the decision is made? Who needs to be informed after it is made? Together, these answers form the RACI structure, named for the four roles: responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed. It is borrowed from broader risk practice. Here it is applied to AI-specific decisions and is not left as a generic template.

Take a deployment decision under Chapter 8’s readiness dimensions, the conditions a deployment must meet to count as ready. The deploying business unit might be responsible for assembling the readiness evidence. It might also be accountable for the underlying use decision. A central AI governance function might hold required concurrence, an approval without which a deployment cannot proceed. The model risk function and legal would then be consulted before the decision. The executive sponsor, the senior leader who backs the deployment, would be informed once it is made.

Required concurrence is a second-line control right. It does not mean that AI governance owns the deployment decision itself. Section 13.6 develops why keeping this distinction sharp matters. A second-line function needs independence to challenge a first-line decision.

Written this specifically, a RACI assignment resolves what a vaguer “governance reviews deployments” leaves open. It states how deployments get reviewed. It states with what authority a reviewer can block one. It states who is left holding responsibility when a deployment goes wrong.

An incident response RACI follows the same logic as the deployment RACI, with different names filling the roles. It is drawn from Chapter 10’s ten-stage lifecycle. It needs one correction that the deployment-decision RACI does not need.

Legal and privacy review cannot sit ahead of notification as a sequential gate, a step that must finish before the next one can start. For a deployment, “consulted before the decision” works as such a gate. For an incident it does not. Chapter 10 names two regulatory clocks, the deadlines for reporting to a regulator. They are Article 73’s provider outer limits, the longest a provider may take to report, and Article 26’s deployer duties. These clocks are not yet in force for most systems. The obligations tied to the high-risk uses listed in the Act’s Annex III take effect 2 December 2027, checked against the consolidated EU AI Act as of 2026-09-12. Only from that date do both clocks run. They run from the moment of awareness, the moment the organization learns of the incident. They do not start when an internal consultation finishes.

Different incidents also route to different legal actors and jurisdictions. The route depends on the organization’s role and the incident type.

Table 13.3 states how the four roles then divide for an incident.

RACI role Who What they do
Responsible The team operating the system Runs the containment step, the step that stops the harm from spreading. They are the ones positioned to halt the system. For an agentic system, they can trigger the circuit breaker that Chapter 12 described, the control that stops the agent.
Accountable The AI governance function Confirms the provisional severity classification, the first rough rating of how bad the incident is. Signs off on the owned, dated remediation from the post-incident review, the review done after the incident is over. Each fix in that remediation carries a named owner and a due date.
Consulted Legal teams and, where personal data is involved, privacy teams Work in parallel with containment and investigation, not ahead of them. Identify the applicable legal role and jurisdiction. Prepare whatever notification the clock requires. That includes an initial report that is incomplete, a first report filed before all the facts are in, where the rules allow it. They do not wait for a complete investigation to finish first.
Informed The executive sponsor and, for the most severe tier, the board Informed once containment is underway, both at the same point. Neither waits for the investigation to close.

Table 13.3. The incident-response RACI. Consultation and notification run beside containment, not ahead of it.

The same four-role structure applies to a deployment decision and to an incident response. The timing differs. RACI states who does what. It does not, by itself, state when. An organization applying it needs to recognize where the incident-response version’s timing constraints differ from the deployment version’s.

13.6 The three lines model

The three lines model comes from risk management in general. Here it is mapped onto AI governance. The model used is the current statement of it from the Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA). The statement was updated 8 July 2026 and checked against that update on 2026-09-12. It describes roles and not department names.

The IIA is explicit that the “lines” name complementary roles and relationships. They operate at the same time. They are not a rank order. They are not an org chart. They are not a sequence of checkpoints that one decision passes through in turn. That framing holds even where an example, like the deployment sequence in section 13.5, shows roles acting at different points in time.

The first line owns and manages risk directly. It is the teams that build, select, and deploy AI systems. They make the day-to-day decisions that earlier chapters describe. They bear primary accountability for those decisions being sound.

The second line provides oversight, standards, and challenge. It does not take over the first line’s underlying decision. An AI governance function occupies this line. It sets standards. It reviews deployments against them. It holds a required-concurrence right where section 13.5 assigned one. It maintains the aggregate visibility that section 13.4 showed a federated program can otherwise lose. It often does this alongside a model risk management function. That function handles the technical validation piece specifically.

A concurrence right that can stop a deployment is a second-line control. It is not evidence that the function has moved to the first line. The two lines stay distinguishable. One owns the underlying business decision. The other can block it on risk grounds. In the Meridian case below, the deploying business unit stays the first-line owner. The governance function’s sign-off is a second-line control. A governance program that treats “has veto power” as a signal of first-line ownership has lost the independence that the second line’s challenge role depends on.

The third line provides independent assurance to the board and senior leadership. Assurance here means confirmation that the first two lines are functioning as designed. It comes from internal audit. Internal audit tests the governance program itself and not any individual AI system. Its mandate is independent of both the teams deploying systems and the function governing them.

Two functions that sit side by side are not automatically “the two lines.” This applies to a model risk management function and an AI governance function. Whether they occupy the same line or different lines depends on each one’s actual role in a given decision. That role needs to be stated explicitly and not assumed from the function’s name.

Agentic systems add pressure to this allocation of lines. The pressure already exists. Agentic systems increase its frequency, speed, and scope. They do not invent it from nothing.

Predictive systems already require ongoing first-line monitoring and outcomes analysis, the study of the results a model’s decisions produce. Current model risk management guidance calls for both. Drift, misuse, and process overrides can all surface after deployment. Drift here means a change in how the model itself performs, a different thing from the classification drift above. A process override happens when someone bypasses a required step. None of these problems waits for a scheduled review.

What changes with an agentic system is the timing of design. Chapter 12’s permission scope, the set of actions the agent may take, and its runtime intervention, the ability to step in while the agent runs, have to be designed before the agent acts. They cannot be reviewed after a single output. A first line that owns day-to-day operation of an agent is, in effect, exercising a risk decision every time the agent takes an action within its granted scope. That happens at a frequency a predictive system’s periodic monitoring cycle does not approach.

The second line’s job shifts correspondingly. It moves from periodic deployment review toward two tasks. One is setting standards for which permission scopes are acceptable. The other is auditing the action logs that Chapter 12 required, the records of each action an agent took. A governance program that has not adjusted its second-line practice for this shift will review agentic deployments with the same pre-deployment checklist. That checklist was built for systems that only ever produced a discrete output to review.

Chapter 11 and Chapter 12 name controls along different axes. The difference matters for tracing a specific control back to a specific owner. Without that tracing, the connection between a technical safeguard and the role accountable for it gets lost.

Table 13.4 sets the three classification vocabularies side by side.

Vocabulary Basis Categories
Chapter 11 Classifies a control by its effect Reducing likelihood, detecting, containing before release, correcting, and transferring residual risk
Chapter 12 Classifies a control by where it sits in the pipeline, the chain of steps a system’s work passes through Inline enforcement, near-real-time detection, aggregate monitoring, and retrospective audit
A third vocabulary Names controls Prevent, detect, contain, intervene, recover, verify, and learn

Table 13.4. Three vocabularies that classify the same controls.

An inline enforcement point, for instance, can carry a preventive effect, stopping a problem, a detective one, finding a problem, or both. It depends on what it is configured to do.

The underlying obligation is the same, whichever axis names the control. Each control still needs a first-line owner who runs it. It needs a second-line function that sets its standard and challenges it. Where the control failed and the failure was material, it needs a third-line assurance check that the standard itself is working.

Chapters 11 and 12 describe controls such as a registry record, a permission scope, and a claim-verification step. Any of these left without a named role is one of the decisions that the RACI and three lines structure has to assign. That structure does not tolerate leaving it unassigned. The gap is the one the structure exists to close.

Which operating model an organization picks determines who ends up in which line for a given decision. It does not only shape how authority is drawn on an org chart. Table 13.2 returns to the operating-model choice that section 13.1 opened. The roles and lines now give that choice concrete stakes.

It is this book’s own synthesis of the tradeoffs that sections 13.1 through 13.6 developed. The table rests on a contingency principle, meaning an idea that the right answer depends on circumstances. Here the principle is that no single structure fits every organization. That principle follows the Federal Reserve/OCC/FDIC’s SR 26-2 supervisory guidance on model risk management (issued 17 April 2026, superseding SR 11-7, checked 2026-09-12) and the GOVERN function, the part that covers governance, of NIST AI RMF 1.0 (checked 2026-09-12). The Federal Reserve, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) are U.S. bank regulators. Read the “risk to control” column across all three rows before choosing any one model. Do not read any row as a fixed or guaranteed outcome. Every operating model carries a predictable risk. The choice is which risk an organization is best positioned to control given its own scale and deployment speed. It is not a search for a model with no risk at all.

13.7 Governance bodies

A governance body is a committee convened to review or approve AI systems. Composition, mandate, and decision rights are the necessary starting point. A body needs more than these three stated, however, if it is to function as a governance mechanism and not as a discussion group.

Composition names who sits on the body and why. It ensures that the perspectives a decision requires are present. These are technical, legal, and business perspectives, and, where relevant, representation for the people a system affects. These perspectives should not be assembled from whoever was available. Mandate states what the body is empowered to decide. This is distinct from what it is only invited to discuss. Decision rights specify whether the body’s decision is binding, advisory, or escalates to someone else.

Consider a committee that spends an hour debating a deployment. Then it watches the business unit proceed regardless. That committee has decision rights of zero, however substantive its discussion was.

Naming these three does not yet make a body able to operate on an ordinary working day. A working governance body needs rules for what comes in, rules for how it decides, and rules for what happens after a decision. Table 13.5 lists that operating detail, grouped by stage.

Stage Element Requirement
What comes in Intake criteria Say what reaches the body and what is handled below it
What comes in Quorum requirement Sets how many members must be present to decide
What comes in Conflict-of-interest check Covers members with a stake in the specific decision
What comes in Evidence pack Assembled by a submitter, the person bringing the proposal, before the meeting
How it decides Decision standard Must be stated
How it decides Minutes The written record of the meeting. Must record dissent as well as the outcome
How it decides Conditional-approval option Approves a system on stated conditions, with a stated exception duration, meaning how long the exception lasts
How it decides Escalation path A route that sends the matter to a higher authority, for when the body cannot reach a decision
After a decision Appeal route For a submitter who disagrees with the outcome
After a decision Action tracking For anything the approval was conditioned on
After a decision Re-review trigger For a material change to the approved system
After a decision Emergency route For a decision that cannot wait for the body’s normal cadence, its regular meeting rhythm
After a decision Periodic review Of whether the body’s own decisions are holding up in practice

Table 13.5. The operating detail a working governance body needs beyond composition, mandate, and decision rights.

A committee may have a charter that names its composition, mandate, and decision rights. Without this operating detail, it still functions ceremonially, meaning for show. It approves whatever evidence happens to be presented to it. It does not govern the decision.

One documented illustration shows a governance body being stood up at close to this level of structural specificity. It does not reach every element listed above. It comes from outside banking. The White House Office of Management and Budget issued Memorandum M-25-21 on 3 April 2025. It rescinded, meaning withdrew, the prior administration’s earlier memorandum, M-24-10. The memorandum requires the head of each U.S. federal agency to retain or designate a Chief AI Officer within sixty days. A Chief AI Officer is the official in charge of an agency’s AI work.

The memorandum separately requires each agency covered by the Chief Financial Officers Act to convene an AI Governance Board within ninety days. The requirement does not cover every federal agency. An existing governance body may serve that function. The memorandum specifies the board’s chair, vice-chair, and required functional representation. Alongside these, a cross-agency Chief AI Officer Council coordinates AI governance across the federal government as a whole. OMB itself convenes the council (checked 2026-09-12).

The memorandum states a requirement. It does not prove that every covered agency met it. The U.S. International Trade Commission’s own AI page reports that it re-designated its Chief AI Officer and retained its AI Governance Board. The page names the board’s thirteen member positions. This is direct evidence that the requirement was implemented and not only imposed (checked 2026-09-13). Neither source specifies the quorum, evidence-pack, or dissent-recording detail that a body needs in order to function. That is the gap between naming a governance body and operating one.

13.8 Acceptable use and sanctioned tooling

Unapproved AI use means employees using AI tools that the organization has not reviewed or approved. It often happens because a sanctioned alternative, a tool the organization has approved, is slower or less capable. Sometimes no sanctioned alternative exists at all. An acceptable use policy, the document telling employees which AI uses are allowed, exists to address this practical problem.

Current government guidance on this pattern was published 7 September 2026. It recommends four steps: understanding what need drove the unapproved use, building a positive security culture, meaning a workplace culture that supports secure behavior, offering an approved alternative, and taking direct steps that reduce the risk. It does not treat a written prohibition as sufficient on its own. A prohibition may be necessary for particular data, tools, or uses. That does not mean every prohibition backfires. Consistent with that guidance, policy text alone rarely creates the visibility or the safe alternative an organization needs. A prohibition with nothing else beside it risks losing visibility into use the organization cannot detect. It does not eliminate the use itself.

A fuller acceptable use policy combines these parts:

  • Clear use rules, with an approved tool that meets legitimate needs where one can be provided.
  • Access and data-loss controls in proportion to the risk. Data-loss controls are tools that stop sensitive data from leaving the organization.
  • Procurement and security review of new tools before they spread informally.
  • Awareness training and a reporting channel for tools employees want but do not have.
  • Monitoring consistent with law and internal policy.
  • A defined response path for discovered unapproved use that does not default to discipline alone.
  • Periodic measurement of how much unapproved use remains, so the policy’s effect can be assessed and not assumed.

Combining all of this into a policy still leaves an individual employee needing one place that states these things concretely. That place should state:

  • Which tools and accounts are approved.
  • Which tasks and data are permitted, restricted, or prohibited by classification. Classification here means how sensitive the data is.
  • Where human review of AI output is required before anyone relies on it.
  • How output accuracy gets verified.
  • How intellectual property, confidentiality, and privacy obligations apply to AI-assisted work.
  • What security requirements attach to an approved tool.
  • What records the employee must keep.
  • What disclosure the organization owes a counterparty or regulator about AI-assisted output. A counterparty is the other side of a deal or contract.
  • How to report an incident or a tool the policy does not cover.
  • How an exception gets requested and granted.
  • What happens when the policy is violated.
  • How often the policy gets checked against the tools employees are asking for.

13.9 Conflict with incumbent functions and executive engagement

Where a model risk management function already exists with real authority, a new AI governance function arrives as a structural competitor and not a blank slate. This was the situation at Meridian. Its model risk management function had real authority for a decade before FAIRLEND’s AI governance function was chartered. Pretending otherwise produces unresolved friction.

The boundary a new function should draw is not the one an assumption of empty incumbent territory would suggest. Current U.S. federal banking supervisory guidance on model risk management was issued April 2026 and supersedes the prior 2011 guidance. It directly addresses model purpose, intended and actual use, limitations, misuse, validation, and monitoring. It also addresses aggregate exposure, meaning risk viewed across models together rather than one at a time, and third-party models, those built by outside vendors. So an incumbent function governed by that guidance already has a real, if partial, mandate over questions of use appropriateness, meaning whether a model is appropriate for a given use. Its mandate is not limited to technical accuracy. A new AI governance function that assumes otherwise is working from an outdated picture of what model risk management is built to check.

That guidance leaves four gaps. It does not cover these:

  • Fundamental-rights obligations, covering the basic legal rights of people, and disclosure obligations.
  • Contestability, which is the ability of affected people to challenge a decision.
  • Affected-party requirements broader than model performance.
  • Governance of the system classes outside the guidance’s stated coverage.

It leaves those gaps for two reasons. It addresses model risk specifically and not every AI-specific legal or social-impact question. It also explicitly excludes generative and agentic models from its own scope.

The correct starting point is therefore a joint-responsibility analysis. The analysis has three steps. It maps what the incumbent mandate already covers. It identifies the AI-specific duties the mandate does not cover. It assigns explicitly the decisions that fall in both territories. This is different from a new function asserting or conceding a three-question territory, a patch of ground marked out in advance by a fixed list of questions. Neither the incumbent’s actual mandate nor its actual gaps line up with that territory.

This specific conflict is banking-specific, and so is the supervisory guidance that resolves its boundaries. A nonfinancial organization can face the same structural pattern. A new AI governance function may meet an established data governance board or cybersecurity risk committee. That body has its own reporting line and its own settled scope. The organization runs the identical joint-responsibility analysis described above. No comparable federal supervisory framework, the set of rules a government supervisor applies to the bodies it oversees, already dictates what the incumbent function’s mandate covers. For that reason, the analysis itself transfers. The specific banking citations do not.

Executive and board engagement depends on regular reporting that answers four matters. The first is the aggregate picture that federated governance can lose. The second is the specific questions to ask of any significant deployment. The third is whether the readiness dimensions that Chapter 8 requires were evaluated. The fourth is whether an incident this year exposed a systemic gap and not an isolated failure.

What a board’s oversight duty requires depends on the entity’s jurisdiction and legal form. But reporting built to answer these questions is what lets a board exercise whatever oversight duty applies to it. Without it, the board receives reassurance dressed as reporting, regardless of which duty governs.

Cadence, how often reporting happens, matters as much as content. No single cadence is the correct one for every board. A board may rely on annual reporting alone, with no operational monitoring or event-driven escalation underneath it. Such a board can learn about a systemic gap long after it opened and not while it is still forming. That lag is too slow for a governance function whose own inventory Chapter 3 requires to be current continuously. Annual reporting paired with the continuous monitoring and escalation triggers described below does not carry the same lag. So the problem is the missing pairing. The annual cadence is not the problem by itself.

Quarterly reporting is one cadence some boards already use for other risk reporting. A similar rhythm for AI governance can let a function surface three things. One is a drifting classification pattern. Another is a rising rate of near-miss incidents, meaning incidents that almost caused harm. The third is a business unit consistently pushing deployment decisions past the readiness bar. The function can surface these while the pattern is still a trend and not an already-realized failure.

Whatever the routine cadence, it needs to be paired with event-driven escalation. Event-driven escalation sends defined triggers to the board or the relevant committee immediately. The triggers are a serious incident, a threshold breach, meaning a measure crossing a set limit, and a material change. They do not wait for the next scheduled report. A board relying on a fixed cadence alone learns about an urgent problem only as fast as its calendar allows.

The report itself should name what changed since the last one. It should not restate the program’s structure every cycle. A board that reads the same description of the operating model repeatedly has learned nothing new about whether the model is working.

Case in focus: a mandate that had to be negotiated, not assumed

FAIRLEND · Hypothetical, following the running cases

This continuing fictional scenario illustrates the conflict pattern that section 13.9 describes. It does not report an event that occurred at any specific organization.

Meridian’s board chartered its AI governance function with a mandate spanning fairness, transparency, and risk review across every AI system in the organization. On its face, the charter read as complete. The model risk management function governed FAIRLEND’s credit models under a supervisory framework it had operated under for years. It read the same charter as an intrusion into territory that was already its own. The function had held that territory without dispute since before “AI governance” was a phrase anyone at Meridian used.

The first several deployment reviews under the new charter produced that pattern. Both functions showed up to review the same model. Each was unclear on whose decision controlled the outcome. Each carried a different picture of what the other’s mandate already covered.

The resolution followed a joint-responsibility analysis. Neither function conceded, and neither asserted exclusive territory. Meridian first mapped what its model risk management function’s existing mandate already covered for FAIRLEND. That mandate was governed by current supervisory guidance. It covered technical validation. It also covered model purpose, intended use, and monitoring for misuse. This was a broader mandate than the AI governance function had initially assumed.

AI governance then identified the specific gaps that the mandate did not close. There were two. The first was whether the population that a proposed lending-product extension would newly reach matched the population that FAIRLEND’s fairness testing had covered. The second was whether Meridian could satisfy the adverse-action explanation duties that Chapter 2 introduces and produce the decision-level evidence that Chapter 15 develops. Adverse-action explanation duties are the duties to explain to a customer why a lender turned them down. Chapter 8 makes both matters conditions of deployment readiness. It is not the source of the underlying legal duty. Both questions required AI-specific and legal expertise. The model-risk mandate did not claim to provide that expertise.

Meridian did not assign these questions as separate, walled-off territories. It required both functions’ sign-off on any deployment touching either question. Model risk management’s validation evidence fed directly into the readiness record. AI governance’s concurrence decision required that record.

Part of the resolution was working out which line each function occupied in the three lines model. Meridian did not assume the two functions were automatically “the two lines” because two functions existed. Both turned out to sit in the second line for FAIRLEND’s deployment decisions. One provided technical challenge. The other provided AI-specific and fairness challenge. In both cases, the deploying business unit remained the first-line owner of the underlying decision.

A stalled product launch forced this mapping into being. It carried a cost that the board tracked directly. The cost had three parts. The first was the delayed launch. The second was that a business unit sponsor, rather than repeat the experience, learned to route around both functions on the next deployment. The third was that the boundary existed on paper only because a deployment had already stalled to force it into being.

Meridian drew a lesson from this, and it is the right lesson to draw. The joint-responsibility mapping should happen before the first contested deployment and not during it. A boundary drawn under the pressure of a stalled launch is drawn worse than one drawn in advance. It carries more resentment on both sides. It gets less attention to whether the mapping tracks what each function’s mandate does and does not already cover.

Summary

Centralized, federated, and hybrid operating models are design patterns and not a performance ranking. The right choice depends on organizational scale, existing risk maturity, deployment speed, risk concentration, and independence requirements. The choice must be tested against an organization’s own conditions. It must not be assumed from any one pattern’s general reputation.

The operating model choice recurs at every governance decision point. It is not made once on an org chart. An organization that has not noticed this is often centralized on one axis and federated on another by accident and not by design.

One candidate split divides authority by layer. It is federated at the system layer, where local context matters most. It is centralized at the model layer, where a defect needs to be caught once and not rediscovered independently. Most individual decisions this split touches are cross-layer processes. They need a decision-rights matrix and not a two-column allocation.

Federated governance fails through classification drift, capture by the business a reviewer serves, and lost aggregate visibility. The last is a serious failure. At least some regulatory reporting obligations attach to the organization, or to a specific provider or deployer role, and not to a single business unit.

RACI applied to governance decisions makes responsibility, accountability, consultation, and information explicit and not assumed. An incident-response RACI needs one adjustment. Its consultation step runs in parallel with containment and investigation. It is not a sequential gate ahead of a regulatory notification clock.

The three lines model, fully mapped onto AI-specific functions per the IIA’s current statement, separates three roles. The first line owns. The second line provides oversight and challenge. This includes a required-concurrence right that does not by itself make the second line an owner. The third line provides independent assurance.

A governance body needs composition, mandate, and decision rights stated explicitly. It also needs operating detail that makes it function and not only exist. That detail includes intake criteria, quorum, evidence pack, dissent record, escalation, and re-review triggers. An acceptable use policy addresses unapproved AI use only by combining a sanctioned alternative, where one can be offered, with access controls, monitoring, training, and measurement. A written prohibition alone does not do this.

Conflict with an incumbent function like model risk management is structural. It cannot be avoided. Resolving it requires a joint-responsibility analysis and not an assumed empty territory. An incumbent function governed by current supervisory guidance may already have real authority over use appropriateness and not only technical validation. The first step is to map what its mandate already covers. The second is to identify the AI-specific duties it does not cover. The third is to assign the overlap explicitly. Neither function concedes or asserts exclusive ground.
