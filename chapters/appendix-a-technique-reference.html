Appendix A: Technique Reference

The chapters apply several general information-gathering and analysis techniques without teaching them from first principles. This appendix is a pointer to what each technique is, when a chapter uses it, and where to look for a fuller treatment, not a substitute for learning any of them properly. Ordinary versions of most techniques assume a cooperative subject, and governance work frequently does not have one. Two techniques get real, worked treatment in their chapters instead of a pointer here for exactly that reason, consulting stakeholders who cannot be reached (Chapter 14, section 14.2) and questioning a vendor who has reason not to answer (Chapter 16, section 16.3). Look there, not here, for those two.

Affinity grouping. Sorting a large number of individually generated ideas, findings, or complaints into a smaller number of natural categories by inspecting them for similarity, not by imposing a category scheme in advance that the raw material might not actually fit. Use it after a brainstorming session or an open-ended survey has produced more individual items than a group can usefully discuss one at a time. See Chapter 3’s shadow AI discovery, where individually reported tools have to be grouped before they can be classified. For further reading, see any general text on qualitative data analysis.

Benchmarking. Comparing a system, a metric, or a process against a defined reference point, either an industry norm, a prior version of the same system, or a stated target, to establish whether a result is good, typical, or concerning. Use it when a number needs context to be interpretable on its own. See Chapter 7’s testing thresholds and Chapter 9’s drift baselines. For further reading, see any general text on quality measurement or statistical process control.

Brainstorming. Structured group generation of options or possibilities before evaluating any of them, deliberately separating idea generation from idea judgment so early criticism does not suppress options a group would otherwise have surfaced. Use it when a risk list, a use case list, or a set of candidate controls needs to be wide before it needs to be right. See Chapter 3’s inventory discovery and Chapter 14’s risk identification. For further reading, see any general text on group facilitation or creative problem solving.

Checklist review. Verifying a deliverable or a decision against a predefined, fixed list of required elements, catching omissions a less structured review would miss. It suits anything with a stable, enumerable set of requirements, such as a documentation package or a release gate. See Chapter 8’s deployment readiness review and Appendix C’s templates generally. For further reading, see any general text on quality assurance methods.

Decision matrix. A table scoring a small number of candidate options against a fixed set of weighted criteria, producing a total score per option that makes the basis for a comparison explicit instead of leaving it implicit in a group’s unstated intuitions. A weighted total is only as sound as the assumptions behind it. It assumes the scales across criteria are comparable and that a high score on one criterion can offset a low score on another. That assumption does not hold for a legal, safety, or rights requirement, which a low overall score should never be allowed to outweigh. Use it whenever more than two options are genuinely under consideration, the criteria that matter can be named in advance, and normalized scales are used. Run a sensitivity check to confirm the ranking survives reasonable changes in the weights. Apply any non-negotiable constraint as a gate before scoring, not by folding it into the score itself. See Chapter 6’s model selection factors and Chapter 13’s operating model comparison. For further reading, see any general text on multi-criteria decision analysis.

Document analysis. Extracting information from existing written material, policies, contracts, model cards, prior incident reports, rather than generating new information through interviews or observation. Use it before a live consultation whenever the timeline allows, since a question already answered in an existing document should not consume a stakeholder’s time, but do not let it delay input that is urgent on its own terms. An affected person’s immediate concern, an incident still unfolding, or a decision on a short deadline should not wait on a document review that could run in parallel instead. See Chapter 15’s evidence discipline and Chapter 16’s model card interrogation. For further reading, see any general text on qualitative research methods.

Facilitated workshop. A structured group session, run by a neutral facilitator, that brings multiple stakeholders together to reach a shared output, a classification scheme, a risk register, a target operating model, in one coordinated session instead of through separate interviews later reconciled by hand. Use it when the output requires agreement across roles, not aggregation of separate opinions. See Chapter 13’s operating model design and Chapter 17’s requirements gathering. For further reading, see any general text on group facilitation technique.

Five whys. A root cause technique that asks “why” repeatedly against a stated problem, each answer prompting the next why, until the questioning reaches a cause the organization can actually act on rather than a symptom one level removed from it. A single chain of whys tends to find one cause, but an incident, especially one involving people, process, and a system together, more often has several interacting contributory factors. Framing the technique as a search for the one root cause can end the inquiry too early. Or it can land on an individual’s action as the answer, when the actual conditions that made that action likely deserve equal attention. Use it when an incident’s immediate cause is obvious but its underlying cause is not. Treat multiple branches as a normal outcome, not a failure of the technique, and direct the questioning at conditions and system design instead of at assigning blame to a person. See Chapter 10’s incident investigation. For further reading, see any general text on root cause analysis, including fishbone or cause-and-effect diagramming as a related technique.

Focus group. A moderated group discussion with a small number of participants selected for a shared characteristic relevant to the question, used to surface reactions, concerns, or usability problems that individual interviews might not reveal through group dynamics alone. Use it when the goal is understanding a shared experience, not gathering independent facts. See Chapter 8’s pilot feedback and Chapter 17’s rollout evaluation. For further reading, see any general text on qualitative research methods.

Gap analysis. Comparing a current state against a defined target state and naming the specific, itemized distance between them, rather than describing either state in isolation. It fits whenever a target has been defined and the question becomes what stands between here and there. See Chapter 17’s current-to-target sequencing. For further reading, see any general text on organizational change management.

Interview, structured and semi-structured. A one-on-one or small-group conversation using a prepared set of questions, asked consistently across participants in a structured interview or adapted in response to what a participant says in a semi-structured one. Use a structured interview when comparability across participants matters most; use a semi-structured one when depth on an individual participant’s specific situation matters more than strict comparability. See Chapter 3’s inventory interviews, Chapter 14’s stakeholder consultation, and Chapter 17’s requirements gathering. For further reading, see any general text on interview technique in applied research.

Observation. Watching how people actually perform a task, in its ordinary setting, instead of asking them to describe it afterward, on the reasoning that a description of a workflow and the workflow itself frequently diverge without anyone intending to mislead. Use it whenever a workaround or an informal practice is suspected but has not been reported. See Chapter 17’s requirements gathering from what people do when a process becomes inconvenient. For further reading, see any general text on contextual inquiry or ethnographic method.

Peer review. Having a person with comparable expertise, but no direct authorship stake in the work, examine a decision, a document, or a finding before it is finalized. The reasoning is that a second qualified reader catches errors the original author’s own familiarity with the material makes hard to see. Reach for it when a finding will be relied on downstream, a classification decision, a risk score, an audit result, and a single unreviewed judgment carries more consequence than the added time a second reader costs. See Chapter 17’s discussion of a second reviewer disagreeing with the first as an outcome metric for whether classification is accurate. For further reading, see any general text on quality assurance in professional practice.

Prioritization scale. A method for ranking a list of items, risks, requirements, findings, into a small number of tiers by relative importance, forcing an explicit comparison instead of leaving every item marked equally important. A ranking assumes the items compared are the kind of thing capacity can trade off against each other. That is not true of a legal, safety, or rights duty, which the organization has no discretion to deprioritize regardless of where it lands on the list. Exclude those items from the ranking exercise, and treat them as required, not just highly ranked. Use it whenever the remaining, genuinely discretionary list is longer than an organization’s capacity to act on all of it at once, which is nearly always. See Chapter 14’s forced risk-ranking tiebreak and Chapter 15’s requirement priority field. For further reading, see any general text on requirements prioritization technique.

Process mapping. Diagramming the sequence of steps a workflow actually follows, including decision points and handoffs between roles, to make a process visible and discussable instead of implicit in individual habit. A map limited to steps and handoffs omits the evidence, controls, exceptions, and ownership a governance process also needs; add those elements before treating the map as complete. Use it before redesigning any workflow, so the redesign changes a documented reality rather than an assumed one. See Chapter 17’s control and workflow design. For further reading, see any general text on business process modeling.

RACI matrix. A table assigning, for each significant activity, exactly who is Responsible for doing the work, who is Accountable for the outcome, who must be Consulted before a decision, and who merely needs to be Informed after one. It resolves the ambiguity that produces both duplicated effort and dropped responsibility. “Exactly one accountable person per activity” is the matrix’s own operating convention rather than a universal organizational rule. It needs reconciling with a governing body’s oversight accountability, an executive’s ultimate accountability, and a control or assurance function’s independent accountability for checking the work, none of which a single RACI row is built to represent on its own. Use it whenever more than one role could plausibly own a task and the organization has not yet said which one does, alongside whatever governance and assurance accountability already sits above the activity level. See Chapter 13’s incident-response responsibility assignment. For further reading, see any general text on project or program governance.

Risk matrix. A grid plotting likelihood against severity, used to compare and prioritize risks on two dimensions simultaneously instead of collapsing them into a single combined score that hides which factor is driving a risk’s position. The ordinal categories a matrix typically uses, low, medium, high, compress a wide range of actual likelihood and severity into a small number of bins. This can rank two meaningfully different risks identically, or place a low-probability, catastrophic risk in the same cell as a frequent, minor one. Calibrate what each category actually means before using it, and treat a cell placement as a starting point for judgment rather than a finished conclusion. Set an explicit non-compensable threshold, too, such as any risk to life or an irreversible rights harm, that a matrix position alone cannot downgrade. Use it whenever a risk list needs comparison, not only enumeration, alongside a stated uncertainty about where a given risk actually falls. See Chapter 14’s risk scoring. For further reading, see Cox, “What’s Wrong with Risk Matrices?”, Risk Analysis 28(2), 2008, on the technique’s known ranking and categorization limits.

Sampling. Examining a subset of a population, selected either randomly or purposively, when examining the full population is impractical. Random selection on its own does not guarantee a representative or statistically defensible estimate. It requires a clearly defined sampling frame that actually covers the population of interest, a known or estimated probability of inclusion for each unit, and a stated plan for handling nonresponse or missing cases. Where the frame or response pattern is uneven, it also requires weighting to correct for it, together with a reported margin of uncertainty rather than a single point estimate presented as exact. Use random sampling, built this way, when the goal is a defensible estimate across the whole population; use purposive sampling when the goal is understanding specific, known cases of concern, and do not present a purposive sample’s findings as population-representative. See Chapter 9’s sampling-with-adjudication approach to delayed ground truth. For further reading, see any general text on sampling methodology in applied statistics.

Stakeholder mapping. Identifying every person or role with a genuine stake in a decision or a system, and characterizing each by their level of influence over the outcome and their level of interest in it. This lets engagement effort be allocated deliberately, not defaulted to whoever is easiest to reach. Influence and interest alone systematically underweight a person or group who is significantly affected by a system’s outputs but holds little organizational power over it. A mapping that stops there tends to concentrate engagement effort on internal stakeholders who are easy to reach, rather than on the people the system actually affects. Add the population’s expected impact, any heightened vulnerability, relevant rights, and the practical barriers a given group faces to participating at all. Use those added dimensions to correct engagement effort toward low-power, high-impact groups, instead of away from them. Use it at the start of any consultation effort, before deciding who to interview, survey, or convene. See Chapter 14’s stakeholder consultation and Chapter 17’s requirements gathering from developers, business owners, and reviewers. For further reading, see any general text on stakeholder analysis method.

Survey and questionnaire. A structured set of questions distributed to a larger group than an interview practically reaches, trading the depth of an individual conversation for coverage across a wider population. It fits when a finding needs to be established across a population instead of illustrated through individual cases. See Chapter 17’s training completion and rollout feedback collection. For further reading, see any general text on survey design.

SWOT analysis. A structured inventory of an initiative’s internal Strengths and Weaknesses alongside external Opportunities and Threats, used to surface a rounded picture before a decision rather than only the factors that first come to mind. Use it sparingly, only where a genuinely rounded picture, not a risk list alone, is what the decision requires. See Chapter 13’s operating model selection. For further reading, see any general text on strategic planning method.

Timeline reconstruction. Assembling a dated, sequential record of what happened, decision by decision and event by event, from logs, messages, and documents produced at the time rather than from participants’ later recollection of the order events occurred in. Use it for any incident investigation or post-mortem where the sequence of decisions matters as much as the decisions themselves. See Chapter 10’s incident documentation requirement that decisions be captured with timestamps as the response unfolds. For further reading, see any general text on incident investigation method.

Traceability matrix. A table linking each requirement to the specific evidence that demonstrates it was met, making a status claim checkable against a named artifact rather than asserted on memory. Use it wherever a later reviewer, internal or regulatory, will ask an organization to demonstrate rather than simply state that a requirement was satisfied. See Chapter 15, section 15.2, for this book’s full treatment. For further reading, see any general text on requirements management.

Voting and ranking techniques. Methods, including simple ranked voting and weighted-point allocation across a fixed budget, for converting a group’s individual preferences into a single prioritized list without one person’s opinion silently dominating the outcome. As with a prioritization scale, exclude a legal, safety, or rights requirement from the vote entirely rather than letting a group’s preference ranking decide whether the organization does something it has no discretion not to do. Use it, for the genuinely discretionary remainder of a list, after brainstorming has produced more candidate items than the group can act on and a shared ranking is needed to choose among them. See Chapter 13’s operating model decision process. For further reading, see any general text on group decision-making method.
