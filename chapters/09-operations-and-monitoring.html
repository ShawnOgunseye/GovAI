9. Operations and Monitoring

MEDASSIST was validated at an academic medical center. It was then used at community hospitals. The gap between the two did not appear on any dashboard the day the model went live. A dashboard is a screen that tracks a system’s numbers. Going live means the system began real use.

The gap first appeared as a handful of nurses at one community hospital noticing a pattern. The model’s alerts seemed to arrive later than their own clinical instinct would have flagged a patient. None of them had the standing or the aggregated data to raise this pattern as anything more than an impression.

Months later, the gap appeared again in a retrospective chart review, which is a look back at patient records after the outcomes are known. A new clinical informatics director, a leader responsible for how a hospital uses clinical data and software, happened to commission it. The review compared alert timing against eventual outcomes across the academic medical center and all six community hospitals. That comparison is where the pattern first became a number, not a feeling.

In between those two moments, the model operated in production, which means real use, as opposed to testing. It generated a steady stream of alerts, most of them unremarkable. A small number were late or missing in the way that mattered. They stayed that way for as long as nobody was watching the specific thing that would have shown it.

Cases like this one happen because a system that enters production keeps operating long after its initial release. That operation produces evidence that no pre-deployment test observed. A pre-deployment test is a test run before release.

Development, release, monitoring, response, and change can overlap instead of running as one clean handoff. This is most likely for a system that is updated continuously. It is also likely for a system retrained on a rolling basis, meaning trained again and again as new data arrive. Watching the system is therefore a continuing loop.

Not every system follows one linear lifecycle. Not every risk first appears after release. Some material harms surface earlier, during data creation, training, evaluation, or predeployment testing. Chapters 5 through 7 have already shown several of them doing so.

What you will be able to do

  • Design a monitoring program across at least five core dimensions, and state what each one is mainly able to detect
  • Choose an approach to performance monitoring when ground truth, the real outcome a prediction tried to anticipate, is delayed, ambiguous, or absent
  • Monitor a generative system, one that creates new text, images, or code, that has no single correct output to check against, and a system that acts, not only outputs
  • Recognize automation bias, the over-trusting of an automated recommendation, from its operational signals and not from a reviewer’s stated intentions
  • Specify what a post-market monitoring plan, the written plan for watching a system after release, must contain as a documented artifact, and not only describe as an activity

9.1 Five monitoring dimensions

A monitoring program built around a single number will miss most of what goes wrong in production. That number is typically accuracy or its nearest proxy, a stand-in measure. Different failure modes surface through different signals. A dimension nobody is watching is a failure mode nobody will catch until it has already caused harm.

Technical monitoring watches whether the system itself is functioning. It tracks latency, error rates, uptime, and integration failures with the systems around it. Latency is how long the system takes to respond. Uptime is the share of time the system is available. Most operations teams already do this well. It resembles conventional software monitoring, and it produces alerts through tools that predate this book’s subject matter.

Performance monitoring watches whether the system’s predictions, generations, or actions remain accurate against whatever ground truth is available. Section 9.2 shows that this is harder than it sounds for the systems this book cares most about.

Fairness monitoring watches whether disparities, meaning gaps in how groups are treated or served, emerge across groups in production. Pre-deployment testing did not reveal them. This matters because the population in production drifts away from the population tested. That can happen well before the aggregate accuracy, the average over all cases, visibly degrades.

Drift monitoring watches whether the statistical relationship between inputs and outputs is changing. The inputs are the data the system receives, and the outputs are what it produces. It does so whether or not the change has yet produced a measurable accuracy drop.

Usage monitoring watches how the system is being used, by whom, and for what. It checks whether that use matches the scope the system was deployed and approved for. It therefore catches scope creep, the slow widening of use beyond the approved purpose. Chapter 8 named scope creep as context alignment’s central risk. Usage monitoring catches it after release, in production, instead of only before deployment.

These five are a minimum view. They are the dimensions most monitoring programs should start with. They are not an exhaustive list, and they overlap instead of sitting in separate lanes. A security incident can show up first as an action-volume anomaly, an unusual jump in how many actions a system takes. A supplier’s silent model change, covered in section 9.6, touches both drift and technical monitoring at once.

A fuller program adds more targets. One is human workflow and oversight, which section 9.8 develops as its own monitoring target. It is not one of the five headline dimensions. A second is security and privacy monitoring specific to what the system newly exposes. A third is change and supply-chain monitoring, which tracks model, configuration, and vendor changes as their own signal. A fourth is impact or complaint signals from the people affected by the system’s decisions.

If a program gives one of these no dimension of its own, the plan should say which of the five covers it. The plan should not leave it uncovered by omission.

Every dimension, whichever list it comes from, needs a response loop. A response loop is a defined path from a triggered threshold to a next step. A threshold is a limit that, once crossed, calls for action. The next step may be triage, corrective action, revalidation, a deployment restriction, incident response, or a governance review. Triage means sorting problems by urgency. Revalidation means testing again that the system still works as approved. A dimension that is watched but produces no defined next action is observed, not monitored.

Figure 9.1 shows five watchpoints that overlap. They are not five independent views of one underlying number. The set is a minimum program, not a complete one.

A system can be technically healthy, performing accurately, and fair by every disaggregated check, meaning a check run separately for each group. Its usage may still have drifted well outside the scope it was approved for. Usage monitoring is well positioned to catch that failure. The other four dimensions are not built to.

A monitoring program that collapses all five into “is the model still accurate” has narrowed a multi-dimensional watch to one dimension. It will be surprised by whichever of the others moves first. A program that stops at these five has the same blind spot in a different place. That happens when it never asks where oversight, security, privacy, and supply-chain risk are being watched.

9.2 Ground truth problems

Performance monitoring assumes access to ground truth, the real outcome a prediction was trying to anticipate. This assumption fails in four distinct ways. They recur across the systems this book follows. Each needs a different workaround, not one generic fix.

Delayed truth is the mildest version. The true outcome exists and will eventually be known. It does not arrive soon enough to inform a timely monitoring signal. A credit model’s ground truth is whether a loan was repaid. That answer arrives over the life of the loan, months or years after the decision.

So FAIRLEND’s monitoring cannot wait for full ground truth before acting on early warning signs. It has to rely on earlier proxy signals. Delinquency at ninety days, meaning payments ninety days behind, stands in for eventual default. The team must stay explicit that the proxy is not the outcome itself.

Ambiguous truth is harder. The outcome exists and is knowable in principle. Reasonable evaluators still disagree about what it was. Content moderation, the work of deciding which posts a platform allows, has this problem built in. Whether a specific post violated a policy is frequently a matter of interpretation, not a fact.

A monitoring program built as though moderation ground truth were objective will treat evaluator disagreement as system error. The disagreement reflects real ambiguity in the task.

Absent truth is close to MEDASSIST’s problem but not identical to it. When an intervention succeeds, the outcome it was meant to prevent never occurs. That leaves no trace to check the prediction against.

Take a sepsis alert. Sepsis is a dangerous reaction to infection. Suppose the alert leads to early treatment, and the patient does not develop septic shock, a life-threatening stage of sepsis. That produces no confirmed case of the deterioration the alert predicted. A monitoring program cannot compare predictions to outcomes directly here. A correct, well-acted-upon prediction erases the evidence that would have confirmed it.

Intervention-affected truth is the hardest version. It is MEDASSIST’s real problem, stated in full. The truth is absent, and it is also unobservable in principle for the specific patient once the intervention has been taken. Treating the patient changes what outcome gets observed.

The untreated counterfactual is what would have happened to this patient without early treatment. A retrospective chart review cannot look it up. It is a quantity that was never generated at all.

A usable signal can still be recovered, by several routes. One is trial design, where it is ethical and feasible. Another is phased or randomized implementation, which means rolling the system out in stages or by random assignment, so that a comparison group exists. Another is causal-inference methods, which model the counterfactual under stated assumptions. Two more are matched comparisons against patients who did not receive the intervention, and careful process measures. Process measures evaluate whether the intervention was warranted and well executed. They do not try to score its outcome directly.

Every one of these methods carries its own assumptions. None of them reconstructs the individual counterfactual outcome for a specific treated patient with certainty. They estimate a population-level effect, or they evaluate the process. A monitoring program has to say which of those two it is doing. It should not present either as a direct confirmation of the original prediction.

Four approaches recur across these problems. Each has its own limitation.

Sampling with human adjudication draws a manageable subset of cases for expert review. Adjudication means a reviewer decides what the right answer is. A slower, more expensive, but more reliable human decision replaces the ground truth that is not otherwise available. The cost is covering only a sample instead of the full population. In the intervention-affected case, the reviewers adjudicate the process and the available evidence. They cannot adjudicate a counterfactual nobody can observe.

Proxy metrics substitute an earlier or more available signal for the true outcome. They are useful because they are available sooner. The proxy’s known limitations must be stated alongside every number it produces. The proxy should not be treated as equivalent to the outcome it stands in for.

Delayed evaluation waits for ground truth to mature before drawing a conclusion. It gives up timeliness for reliability. It is appropriate where a wrong early signal would cost more than a late correct one.

The fourth approach is causal and comparison-based methods. It is specific to the intervention-affected case. It includes matched comparisons, phased or randomized rollouts, and causal-inference techniques. These answer a population-level question about whether the intervention helps on average. They do not answer a patient-level question about what would have happened to any one person.

MEDASSIST’s community hospital gap was, in the end, confirmed as a performance problem only through a version of the first approach. That version was a retrospective chart review, which adjudicated a sample of cases by hand against the available process evidence. No proxy metric and no wait for later data could substitute for examining what happened to specific patients whose deterioration the model should have caught.

9.3 Monitoring generative output

A generative system, one that creates new text, images, or code, compounds the ground truth problem instead of escaping it. It is not accurate to say a generative system has no ground truth at all. A generation can still be checked against the source documents it was supposed to be grounded in. It can be checked against a citation it claims supports a fact. It can be checked against an executable test, which is a test a computer can run. It can also be checked against a task outcome that a user or a downstream process can confirm.

What is true is narrower. A single, universally correct output to match against is rarely available, as it is for a classifier, a model that sorts items into categories. Monitoring therefore has to shift. It moves from comparison against one correct answer to a family of narrower, partial, and task-dependent checks. In combination they cover more of the output than any one of them alone.

Rubric-based sampling extends Chapter 7’s evaluation discipline into production. A rubric is a scoring guide. The team draws a periodic sample of real outputs and grades them against the same kind of rubric a pre-deployment evaluation used. This catches drift in quality that a static pre-launch evaluation cannot see. Production inputs are not the evaluation set.

Groundedness checking is specific to a retrieval-augmented system, which is a system that looks up source material and uses it to write its answers. The check verifies whether a generated claim is supported by the retrieved source material it was meant to be grounded in. It catches a specific failure. The system fabricates a plausible-sounding elaboration beyond what its own sources support.

Refusal and over-refusal rates track both directions of a different failure. A refusal is the system declining a request. A rising refusal rate can signal the system declining to help with legitimate requests it should be handling. A falling refusal rate on requests that should be declined signals the opposite failure. Watching only one direction misses the other.

User-reported failures are an imperfect signal on their own. They still catch categories of failure that no automated check was designed to look for. The reporting channel has to be easy enough to use that a frustrated user uses it instead of giving up.

This list is a starting set, not the full range a production generative system needs watched. A fuller monitoring coverage map adds several checks. It adds factuality checks against whatever sources are available, citation validity, and privacy leakage in generated output. It adds harmful content, and prompt injection attempts (attempts to trick the system with instructions hidden in its input) and whether they succeeded. It also adds retrieval poisoning, which is tampering with the collection of documents a system draws from. It adds latency and cost, rates of users correcting or appealing an output, and task success where a downstream outcome can be observed at all.

Building the map starts from each system capability and risk. From each one, the team works to a specific signal. Each signal needs a data source, a sampling plan, a threshold, a named owner, and a defined response. The map should also state the blind spot that the signal does not cover. The team should not add checks piecemeal until the list feels long enough.

An aggregate check, one that averages over all outputs, can miss the failures that matter most. The sampling plan should deliberately oversample the categories most likely to contain one. A uniform random sample dilutes them below the level an average can detect.

Aggregate quality metrics conceal the failures that matter, and the reason is built into how averages work. A metric like average output quality, or average groundedness score, is dominated by the large volume of ordinary outputs a generative system produces. The failures a governance function needs to catch are rare and severe, not common and mild. A governance function is the group responsible for oversight. A single fabricated clinical fact buried inside thousands of accurate summaries is an example.

Averaging across a population where failures are rare produces a reassuring number. It looks reassuring because of the way it is computed. A monitoring program that relies on an aggregate average is blind to the tail it most needs to see. The tail means the rare, extreme cases.

9.4 Monitoring agent actions

An agentic system takes actions toward a goal, such as tool calls. Its unit of monitoring is the action it took, not the output it produced. Chapter 7 already made this distinction for testing. Operational monitoring has to carry it forward into production. A system that acts generates a different kind of record than one that only produces outputs.

Making the action the unit of monitoring does not mean ground truth stops mattering. Ground truth becomes one of several things to check alongside the action itself. A wrong result or a harmful effect can still occur even when every action taken was individually authorized and within scope.

Each action needs to be monitored along at least five questions. What was done? Under whose authority was it done? What outcome did it produce, and was that outcome correct or desired? What effect did it have? And did it fall inside the permission scope the system was granted, and meet the postconditions the action was supposed to satisfy? A postcondition is something that must be true after the action finishes.

Monitoring is the operational surveillance layer. It watches what a designed system does once it is running. Designing the permission boundary is a separate task, covered in Chapter 12. So is designing the log format that records the action, covered in Chapter 15.

Scope violation detection watches for an action taken outside the permission boundary the system was granted. The permission boundary is the limit on what the system is allowed to do. This detection catches a permission design that looked sufficient on paper but proves too permissive, or too ambiguous, once real traffic exercises it.

Unexpected tool use detection watches for a pattern of tool calls that, while individually within scope, forms a combination or sequence nobody anticipated when the scope was granted.

Action volume anomaly detection watches for a rate of activity that departs sharply from the pattern the system’s approved use case would predict. That signal caught TALENTSCREEN’s scheduling agent in the case in focus below. It did so well before anyone had traced the anomaly to its specific cause.

None of these three substitutes for preserving a full trace of what the agent did. The trace covers the model’s own messages, and its tool calls and their results. It also covers its internal state, the permissions active at the time, the actions taken, and their outcomes. A scope check or a volume count computed after the trace has been discarded cannot be re-examined when a new question arises about what happened.

Section 9.3 warns that averaging hides rare, severe failures in generative output. Averaging across an agent’s action volume hides the same kind of failure. A monitoring program should instead sample and directly review the categories of action most likely to contain a serious failure. These include duplicate or irreversible actions, actions taken under an unusually broad grant of access, and actions following an unusual tool-output pattern. The program should not trust that a rare failure will show up in an aggregate rate computed across everything the agent does.

Figure 9.2 shows three monitoring practices that differ in how they work. They echo the testing distinction Chapter 7 drew and carry it into production. They are also three columns that a real deployment can draw from more than one of at once.

A predictive system forecasts an outcome. Its monitoring contends with ground truth that is delayed, selectively observed, contested, a proxy for what is meant, or affected by the intervention itself. It leans on sampling, proxies, delayed evaluation, or causal comparison to match each situation.

A generative system’s monitoring rarely has one universally correct output to check against. It substitutes source checks, executable tests, rubric sampling, groundedness checks, human review, and outcome measures as the task allows.

An agentic system’s monitoring watches goals, plans, tool calls, permissions, state, actions, side effects, postconditions, outcomes, and recovery together. It does not watch actions to the exclusion of whether the result was correct.

Suppose a team builds one dashboard modeled on the predictive column. If it points that dashboard at a system that also generates or acts, it watches only a fraction of what that system does in production. A team whose system combines more than one class needs more than one column’s monitoring running at once.

9.5 Fairness monitoring

Fairness testing in Chapter 7 checked a snapshot. It tested the model as it existed at the moment of testing against the population available at that moment. Production is not a snapshot. A disparity that pre-deployment testing did not reveal can emerge months into operation. It can emerge because the deployment population itself shifts. It can also emerge because edge cases, meaning rare and unusual situations, accumulate that a finite test set never sampled densely enough to catch.

Fairness monitoring is more than Chapter 7’s disaggregated analysis rerun on a schedule. Disaggregated analysis breaks results out by group. A monitoring program should be built on the precise fairness definitions, the score-versus-decision distinction, and the impossibility conditions that Chapter 7 states.

Production also raises questions that a one-time pre-deployment test does not have to answer. What denominator is each rate computed against, and over what outcome window? The denominator is the total that a rate is divided by. The outcome window is the span of time in which outcomes are counted. How is the resulting number’s uncertainty treated, given a production sample that may be smaller than a curated test set? What minimum sample size is required before a computed disparity is trusted at all? Is the analysis run at the intersection of more than one protected characteristic, rather than one at a time? A protected characteristic is a trait, such as race or sex, that anti-discrimination law protects. How is a delayed outcome handled without waiting silently for it? And how are feedback effects, complaints, and human overrides of the system’s own recommendations factored into what the numbers mean?

A production fairness monitoring plan states these items:

  • the favorable outcome and the positive class it is measuring against
  • the metric and the reason it was chosen
  • the groups being compared
  • the denominator, the observation window, and the minimum sample size
  • how uncertainty below that sample size is treated
  • whether and how intersectional groups are analyzed
  • how delayed outcomes are handled
  • the alert and investigation thresholds
  • the named owner
  • whether legal review is required before a threshold breach becomes public or actionable
  • the mitigation options available
  • the schedule on which the whole plan itself is revalidated

Setting the alert threshold in advance matters for the same reason a red team’s disposition process mattered in Chapter 7. A red team is a group that attacks a system to find its flaws. A threshold decided after seeing a concerning number is vulnerable to being rationalized away by whoever would rather not act on it. A threshold fixed before any concerning number appeared has no such vulnerability built in.

9.6 Drift

Drift is change in the statistical relationship a model was trained to capture. Telling input drift from concept drift is a useful starting diagnosis. It does not dictate a single response, such as a data fix for one and a retrain for the other. Retraining means training the model again on newer data. Treating the diagnosis as if it dictated one response oversimplifies a decision that needs its own process.

Input drift is a change in the distribution of the data a model receives, meaning how its values are spread. The underlying relationship between that data and the correct outcome does not change. Suppose a lending model starts receiving applications with a different income distribution than its training data. If the relationship between income and repayment has not changed, the model is experiencing input drift.

Not every input distribution change deserves the same response. It can be harmless, if the model’s learned relationship holds across the new distribution too. It can be harmful, if it does not. It can be temporary and correct itself without intervention. It can be a symptom of a pipeline fault upstream of the model, not a real change in the population. A pipeline fault is a break in the systems that feed the model data. Or it can be a genuine, lasting signal that the population being served has changed.

A representativeness fix is often the right response. That means making the training data match the people now served. But retraining on a distribution that turns out to be a temporary pipeline artifact can entrench a problem instead of solving it. Telling these cases apart is part of the work. It is not a detail to skip past on the way to a fix.

Concept drift is a change in the underlying relationship itself. The same input now predicts a different outcome than it used to. No amount of representative sampling will fix this, because the thing the model learned is no longer true.

Fraud is an example. Fraud techniques evolve past whatever pattern the model learned to recognize. In that case, retraining on data reflecting the new relationship is usually necessary. But even here retraining is one option among several. A real concept change can call for recalibrating the existing model, which means adjusting its scores to fit current reality. It can call for adding a new feature, an input that captures what changed. It can call for adjusting a decision threshold or rule. It can also call for restricting the system’s use while the relationship is investigated, rolling back to a prior version, or retiring the system. The choice depends on what changed and how much.

Detecting drift and deciding what to do about it are two different steps. Stating them as one two-way fork overstates what either check can promise on its own. In that fork, input drift is detected immediately, and concept drift is detectable only once ground truth arrives. An input change can take time to accumulate to a detectable level. A concept change can sometimes be inferred earlier from a leading indicator, an early warning signal, before full ground truth confirms it.

A more accurate process has five stages:

  • Detect a candidate change. Use enough data, and make an explicit assessment of the statistical uncertainty or power behind the detection, so the team does not react to noise. Power is the ability to notice a change that is really there.
  • Diagnose the source. Common sources are an input distribution change, a change in the actual outcome relationship, and a change in how labels are produced. Labels are the correct answers attached to the data. Others are a pipeline fault, a policy or environment change, and a model version change. The source might also be a prompt change, a change in the retrieval corpus, or a configuration change. The retrieval corpus is the collection of documents the system looks up. These sources call for different owners and different fixes, even when the detected symptom looks similar.
  • Assess materiality, which means how much the change matters. Evaluate it against performance, fairness, safety, and the system’s actual use. Not every detected change needs action.
  • Choose the intervention. Match it to the diagnosis, and do not default to retraining just because that is the response most readily associated with “drift”. Options include recalibration, data repair, retraining, a threshold or rule change, escalation to a vendor, rollback, restriction, or retirement.
  • Validate that the intervention worked. Monitor for recurrence, and preserve the decision record.

One source of change belongs in the diagnosis stage. It is specific to the selection path this book has returned to since Chapter 6. It should not be treated as a third category of data drift.

Take a purchased model. An apparent shift may not come from the deploying organization’s own population at all. The supplier may have updated the model or service underneath the deploying organization’s unchanged interface. An interface here is the connection, often called an API, through which the organization’s software calls the model. This update is a model-version or configuration change, not a new kind of drift. It needs to be checked as its own diagnosis. It should not be folded into “developer-side drift” as though it belonged in the same category as an actual population shift.

An organization that monitors only its own inputs and outputs may not track which model version is running behind its API calls. It can then misdiagnose a supplier-side change as its own population shifting, when nothing about its population changed at all.

Figure 9.3 shows a five-stage process that a detected change moves through. It replaces a two-row lookup table that matched input-versus-concept to data-fix-versus-retrain. Detection flags a candidate change with a stated confidence, not a certainty.

Diagnosis is where the real branching happens. It includes a possibility the two-row version omitted. What looks like a population shift can be a supplier silently changing the model or service behind an otherwise unchanged interface. That is a version or configuration change to check directly, not a third kind of drift.

Materiality assessment and the choice of intervention follow the diagnosis instead of a fixed input-versus-concept rule. Validation closes the loop. It confirms that the intervention worked, instead of assuming it did because it was applied.

9.7 Post-market monitoring plans

A post-market monitoring plan is a documented artifact with defined contents. Post-market means after the system is released. This holds under the EU AI Act, the European Union’s law on AI, and in the broader governance practice this book teaches. The plan is not a description of an activity an organization asserts it performs.

Which organization holds which duty depends on a role. The plan has to state that role before it states anything else.

Article 72 places the post-market monitoring system and plan duty on the provider of a high-risk system. The provider is the organization that builds the system and puts it on the market. A high-risk system is one the Act places in its higher-risk category. The provider must actively and systematically collect, document, and analyze relevant data across the system’s lifetime. This includes the system’s interactions with other AI systems where relevant.

Article 26 places a separate, narrower set of duties on the deployer, the organization that uses the system. These duties include operating the system according to the provider’s instructions. They include assigning competent human oversight with real authority and support, and monitoring the system’s operation. They include suspending use in specified risk circumstances, informing the provider or relevant authorities where required, and retaining logs the deployer controls. Article 73 adds serious-incident reporting on top of both.

An organization writing its own monitoring plan has to say first which of these roles it holds. It may be provider, deployer, or both for different parts of the same system. The contents required differ by role.

A provider’s plan documents continuous compliance analysis across the system’s lifetime, drawing on deployer-reported data and other sources. It also documents the system’s interaction with other AI systems, links to technical documentation and serious-incident reporting, and corrective and preventive action. A deployer’s plan documents operating within instructions, oversight assignment, its own monitoring, and its own reporting and log-retention obligations.

Article 72 originally called for a Commission implementing act specifying a template for the plan, due by February 2, 2026. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 replaced that power with Commission guidance, including a template, due by September 2, 2027. This was checked against two secondary sources on 2026-10-19 and has not yet been checked against the Official Journal text. A regulatory deadline can change again, so the status should be re-verified against the current official text before this section is relied on.

Beyond the legal roles, the plan needs the same defined contents whichever role is writing it. It must name which of section 9.1’s dimensions are being monitored. It must state the method, the schedule, the threshold that triggers escalation, and who is accountable for reviewing the results.

A plan that states an organization “monitors performance regularly” has not documented a post-market monitoring plan. It has documented an intention. The two are not interchangeable. This is true for compliance, and it is true for the more basic governance purpose of catching what section 9.1’s dimensions exist to catch.

A complete plan still establishes only design, not operation. These are two different layers of evidence, and a well-written plan cannot stand in for the second. An auditor reading the plan alone can determine what the organization committed to monitor, by what method, and on what schedule. The auditor cannot determine whether the collection, review, escalation, and response the plan describes happened.

That second layer needs its own operating record. The record holds the data collected, the analyses performed, the alerts reviewed and by whom, the decisions made, the actions completed, and any exception left unresolved. A plan that has never generated that operating record is design without operation, whatever its contents promise.

9.8 Human oversight in practice

Chapter 8 named oversight readiness as a dimension of organizational readiness. Oversight in practice is where a reviewer’s oversight either functions or collapses into a formality. The collapse has specific, detectable signals. A governance function should watch for them as its own monitoring target.

Meaningful oversight exists when a human reviewer has the time, the authority, and the information needed to catch a wrong output and act on it. Nominal oversight exists when a human sits in the review position but works, in practice, as a rubber stamp. A rubber stamp approves whatever it is shown. The volume, the time pressure, or the framing of the task has made real review impractical. This is true regardless of the reviewer’s intentions or competence.

The distinction matters because an organization can point to a human-in-the-loop control that satisfies a compliance checklist. A human-in-the-loop control means a person reviews the system’s outputs. That control may have quietly stopped doing anything a checklist would recognize as oversight.

Automation bias, introduced in Chapter 1 as the tendency to over-trust an automated recommendation, produces detectable operational signals. It does not remain a purely psychological phenomenon invisible to monitoring. Each signal is an investigation trigger, not proof of the underlying failure on its own. Every one of them has an innocent explanation alongside the concerning one.

The first signal is an approval rate approaching unity. This means a reviewer agrees with the system’s recommendation in nearly every case. That pattern is at least as consistent with a reviewer who has stopped making independent decisions as it is with a system that has become extraordinarily accurate. It is equally consistent with an easy population of cases or a policy design that narrows the reviewer’s real discretion. It is also consistent with batching effects, or with an appropriate degree of automation for the task.

The second signal is review times too short to plausibly support the evaluation the role requires. Timestamps a system already logs measure this directly. The third signal is absence of override. This means a reviewer has never once departed from the system’s recommendation across a meaningful volume of cases.

None of the three should be treated as a finding until it has been validated. The validation has several parts:

  • Check a blinded sample, where the reviewer does not know which cases are being checked.
  • Compare against an independent human decision on the same cases, controlling for case complexity and time on task.
  • Check the quality of whatever overrides do occur, not only their count.
  • Account for outcomes that are themselves delayed.
  • Where the pattern persists, interview or directly observe the reviewer. Look at the reviewer’s workload and alert burden, and whether the reviewer had the authority and the information the role assumes.

A governance function that treats an approval rate approaching unity as a finding, without running this validation, has replaced one unverified assumption with another. The first assumption is that the reviewer is engaged. The second is that the reviewer is not.

MEDASSIST’s alert fatigue, introduced in Chapter 1 as a governance concept, is related to automation bias. It is not automation bias running in the opposite direction. The two are distinct mechanisms that happen to produce a mirror-shaped operational signature.

Automation bias is over-trusting what a system recommends. Alert fatigue is a rational-seeming adaptation to volume and workload. It degrades attention regardless of how much the reviewer trusts the system. A nurse managing eleven patients and a high volume of alerts, many of them low-value, can learn to dismiss alerts quickly. The reason has nothing to do with how much she trusts the alert. The real driver is how many of them she has to process.

The detectable signal for alert fatigue is a dismissal rate approaching unity, not an approval rate. It comes alongside review times too short to have evaluated the alert’s content. It needs the same validation discipline as automation bias. Until validated, it is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Once validated, the two mechanisms call for different interventions. Automation bias calls for retraining the reviewers or restructuring incentives. Alert fatigue calls for workload and alert-volume management. The underlying operational discipline is the same. A governance function monitors dismissal and override rates, review timing, and the content of a validated sample of reviewed decisions. That function can catch either failure mode before it produces the specific missed case that first makes it visible. The alternative is waiting, as Northfield, the organization that runs MEDASSIST, did, for a retrospective review to find it after the harm has already occurred.

Case in focus: two signals a dashboard could have caught earlier

MEDASSIST and TALENTSCREEN · Hypothetical, following the running cases

The retrospective chart review that eventually surfaced MEDASSIST’s community hospital gap was not the first opportunity to catch it. It was the first time anyone looked with the right method. Section 9.2’s absent-truth problem meant that no simple comparison of predictions to confirmed outcomes could have caught the gap directly. A prevented deterioration leaves no confirmed case to compare against.

But section 9.8’s automation bias signals were available. These were alert dismissal rates and review timing at each community hospital. They had been quietly diverging from the academic center’s pattern for months before anyone examined them. Nursing staff at the community hospitals received alerts calibrated to a population and workflow the model did not describe. They dismissed a higher proportion of alerts, and dismissed them faster, than staff at the academic center. A monitoring program watching dismissal rates by site would have flagged that pattern for investigation well before the retrospective review became necessary.

Nobody was watching that signal. The monitoring program in place tracked only the technical and aggregate-performance dimensions from section 9.1. It did not track human oversight, the added target that section 9.8 develops. A program that covered only two of the five dimensions, with nothing on human oversight, caught nothing. The harm grew until it was large enough to trigger a review by other means.

Calloway, the organization that uses TALENTSCREEN, discovered an anomaly in the scheduling agent by a cleaner path. The signal that caught it was one the organization had in place. That signal was action volume anomaly detection from section 9.4.

A routine review of the agent’s weekly scheduling volume showed a sharp, unexplained spike at one regional office. The spike was well outside the pattern the tool’s approved use case would predict. Investigation traced the spike to a configuration event. The platform’s own access log had recorded the event, but nobody had reviewed it until the volume anomaly prompted the look.

A local hiring manager had granted the agent access to a broader calendar system than its approved permission scope covered. The manager intended a convenience. The platform accepted the change without an alert comparing the new effective access against the approved scope. Every scheduling action the agent then took passed its own runtime permission check. By that point, the broader access was what the system believed the agent was allowed to use. No single action, evaluated on its own, would have looked wrong. Each one was a normal scheduling decision correctly executed under the access it had.

The scope violation was real, and it was logged. It occurred at the moment the access was expanded beyond what the deployment record approved, not at the moment any scheduling action ran. The action volume signal surfaced it a week later, because nobody had built an alert on the configuration log itself. The anomaly was visible only in aggregate. That is the argument section 9.4 makes for monitoring actions as a population, instead of only auditing them one at a time after a complaint arrives.

Both cases teach the same lesson from opposite directions. MEDASSIST’s harm went undetected because a dimension of monitoring, human oversight, was never built into the program. TALENTSCREEN’s anomaly was caught quickly because the corresponding dimension, action volume, was in place. A monitoring program is only as complete as the dimensions it covers. The gap between five dimensions in principle and two or three in practice is where harm accumulates unseen.

Summary

Operation is the longest phase of a system’s life. It is the phase where most of this book’s risk materializes. A monitoring program built around a single accuracy-like number will miss most of that risk.

Five core dimensions are technical, performance, fairness, drift, and usage. They are a minimum. They are not an exhaustive or mutually exclusive taxonomy. A fuller program also names where human oversight, security and privacy, change and supply-chain, and complaint signals are being watched. A program that reduces everything to performance monitoring alone has narrowed its watch to one dimension without noticing it did so.

Ground truth can be delayed, ambiguous, absent, or affected by the very intervention being evaluated. Four approaches recover a usable signal from these situations. They are sampling with adjudication, proxy metrics with stated limitations, delayed evaluation, and causal or comparison-based methods. MEDASSIST’s intervention-affected problem is the hardest of these cases. It is an unobservable individual counterfactual, which goes beyond absent truth.

Generative systems rarely have one universally correct output to check against. That is not the same as having no ground truth at all. Source checks, executable tests, rubric-based sampling, groundedness checking, human review, and outcome measures each cover part of the gap. A fuller coverage map adds factuality, citation validity, privacy leakage, harmful content, prompt injection, retrieval poisoning, and user-correction signals. Sampling should favor the rare, severe failures that an aggregate average conceals.

Agentic systems require monitoring of goals, plans, tool calls, permissions, state, actions, side effects, postconditions, and outcomes together. Monitoring actions alone, without checking whether the result was correct, is not enough. That means watching for scope violations, unexpected tool combinations, and volume anomalies. It also means preserving full traces and directly sampling the categories most likely to contain a severe failure.

Fairness monitoring builds on Chapter 7’s method. It adds what production requires beyond a snapshot test. These additions are a stated denominator, observation window, minimum sample size, intersectional analysis, delayed-outcome handling, and named ownership. All of them run against thresholds fixed before any concerning number appears.

Drift detection and response is a five-stage process. The stages are detect, diagnose, assess materiality, decide the intervention, and validate. It is not a two-way lookup between input-drift-means-data-fix and concept-drift-means-retrain. A supplier silently changing a purchased model behind an unchanged interface is a version or configuration change to diagnose directly. It is not a third category of drift.

A post-market monitoring plan is a documented artifact. It names dimensions, methods, schedules, thresholds, and accountability. It is not an asserted activity. It has to state first whether the organization holds the EU AI Act’s provider duties, deployer duties, or both, since the required contents differ by role.

Human oversight can collapse from meaningful to nominal in detectable ways. The signals are an approval or dismissal rate approaching unity, review times too short to support real evaluation, and an absence of override. Each is an investigation trigger requiring validation, and none is a finding on its own. Alert fatigue is a related but distinct mechanism from automation bias, and it is not its mirror image.

Evidence carried forward. Monitoring watches the constraints, residual risks, versions, and human workflow that Chapter 8’s release record specified. It does not follow a generic checklist independent of what was decided. It is where a finding gets its first disposition, meaning the first decision about what to do with it. The options are triage, corrective action, revalidation, a deployment restriction, an incident, or a decision to roll back or retire the system. Chapter 10 picks up from that last disposition, incident response, when monitoring surfaces something that needs more than a routine correction.

Monitoring tells an organization something is wrong. It does not, by itself, fix anything. The next chapter turns to what happens after that moment. It covers how a response is classified, staffed, and executed, and what reporting obligations attach and on what timeline. It also covers how a root cause analysis reaches the systemic factors that the case in focus above already illustrates. A root cause analysis is a method for finding the deeper causes of a failure. It does not stop at the first proximate cause, the nearest and most obvious one, that a rushed investigation finds convenient to blame.
