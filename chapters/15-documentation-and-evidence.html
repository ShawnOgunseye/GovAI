15. Documentation and Evidence

Modified

November 16, 2026

Consider a constructed scenario, continuing the running cases. When a regulator asked Northfield Health to demonstrate that MedAssist’s sepsis alert had performed within its validated bounds for the community hospital where a delayed alert contributed to the incident in Chapter 10, Northfield’s governance team went looking for the record. They found that the requirement the model had been built against had no acceptance criteria attached to it. The requirement read, “the model shall flag deteriorating patients with clinically acceptable sensitivity.” Sensitivity is the share of truly sick patients an alert catches, and naming the metric was as far as the requirement went. Everyone who had worked on MedAssist believed the requirement had been met, because the model had performed well in the testing everyone remembered. Northfield’s governance team could produce no record of what “acceptable” meant, what threshold had been tested against it, or when that test had last been rerun for the community hospital population specifically.

The belief was probably correct. For the regulator’s purposes, it was also unusable. A regulator who asks to see evidence cannot act on an organization’s confidence in its own memory. It can only act on what the organization can produce. Where a regulation requires the record to exist independently of the underlying practice, a missing record is its own separate problem, whether or not the practice itself was sound.

Documentation discipline is what turns confidence into evidence, and this book groups that discipline into four parts.

  1. Requirements specific enough to test
  2. A traceability matrix
  3. A model card
  4. Logging

The records that logging produces must be detailed enough to reconstruct a decision or an action months after the fact. Agentic systems make that reconstruction harder, because an agent produces a chain of actions instead of a single output and the record has to cover every step of it. The harder version of the problem belongs to the same discipline. Reconstructing what happened is a different task from reconstructing who is answerable for it.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a requirement specific enough to test, and distinguish a weak version of a requirement from a strong one on sight
  • Build a traceability matrix that connects a requirement to its control (the safeguard meant to meet it) and its evidence. Use it to answer whether a requirement is satisfied or only addressed on paper
  • Read a model card critically. Treat an omission or a vague section as an unresolved evidence question to raise. It is not an incidental gap, and it is not a settled fact about what happened
  • Specify logging that can reconstruct a decision or an agentic action. Include the authority chain (the record of what permission an agent acted under), which a decision log alone cannot supply. Then separate that chain from the question of who is legally and organizationally accountable

15.1 Writing testable requirements

A testable requirement’s normative statement needs five elements to be verifiable rather than aspirational.

  1. The actor or system the requirement binds
  2. The behavior required of it
  3. The operating conditions and the population the requirement applies to
  4. A measurable criterion
  5. A method for verifying that criterion

The requirement’s record carries more fields, and they matter for managing the requirement rather than for testing it. A stable identifier lets people reference and trace it. A source records where the requirement came from. A priority says what happens when it competes with another requirement for resources. An owner and a status complete the record, and the status says whether the requirement is currently met, partially met, unverified, or not applicable. A requirement that will be reused or revised also carries a version and its dependencies on other requirements. Northfield’s sensitivity requirement had no measurable criterion and no verification method, the two elements that make testing possible. That is not a weaker version of a complete requirement. It is a statement of intent with no way to tell whether the intent was met.

Measurability alone does not make a requirement sound. A number attached to a requirement is only as good as the evidence that produced it. A clinical threshold, a testing cadence, or a population boundary needs justification from clinical evidence, applicable regulation, and the system’s own validation record, not from how precise it sounds on the page. Here is a weak version of Northfield’s requirement. It reads, “the model shall flag deteriorating patients with clinically acceptable sensitivity.” It sounds specific because it names a metric, sensitivity. But it names no threshold and no population. Two reviewers applying the same words in good faith could reach different conclusions about whether a given result satisfies it, and the requirement leaves that disagreement where it stands. “Clinically acceptable” carries real meaning to a clinician. That meaning was never turned into something a test can check against.

A strong version states the threshold and the population together. It also traces each number to where it came from. For example, it reads, “the model shall achieve sensitivity of at least [the threshold established in the device’s own validation record] for sepsis onset within [the detection window that record specifies], measured per hospital site, retested on [the cadence the record’s monitoring plan requires].” The bracketed form is deliberate. A real requirement fills each bracket from a specific clinical or regulatory source and records that source in the requirement’s evidence field. The validation record supplies the threshold and the detection window, and the monitoring plan it names supplies the cadence. A requirement that states a number with no traceable origin has replaced one unverifiable claim with a more convincing-looking one. A single sensitivity threshold is also not the whole of what a real acceptance decision needs. A clinical alert can hit a sensitivity target while still producing an unusable false-positive burden, poor calibration, or a workflow effect that makes clinicians tune it out. A complete requirement pairs sensitivity with the complementary measures the specific decision calls for, specificity, positive predictive value, calibration, or alert burden among them, instead of resting on one metric alone. What makes the strong version strong is that it can fail a test against a stated, sourced criterion, not that it contains numbers. The weak version cannot fail, because nothing was specified in enough detail to fail against. A requirement that cannot fail a test has not constrained anything.

15.2 Traceability

A traceability matrix links each requirement to the control that addresses it and the evidence that the control works, and its purpose is answering three questions a requirements list alone cannot. Is anything addressing this requirement? What specifically addresses it? Has that thing been verified to work, by whom, and as of when? Table 15.1 shows what a matrix with real rows looks like for two requirements of FAIRLEND, Meridian’s credit system.

Table 15.1. Traceability matrix for two FAIRLEND requirements

ID Requirement Control Evidence Status
FL-014 Adverse action notice must state the creditor’s actual principal reasons for a denial, per Regulation B, 12 CFR 1002.9 Explanation module generates factor-ranked output at denial time Sample of 50 denial explanations reviewed in Q2 2026; no sampling protocol, tolerance, or comparison method against the model’s actual decision logic was recorded Unverified. Evidence exists but does not establish that the stated factors are the actual principal reasons
FL-015 Explanation must be understandable to a layperson, not merely technically accurate Explanation module output reviewed against a plain-language readability standard Readability scoring on the same 50-sample set; no comprehension or usability testing with actual readers was performed Unverified. A readability score measures grade level, not whether a reader actually understood the explanation

Neither requirement can be marked “met” from the evidence recorded, and the reason differs for each. FL-014 turns on what Regulation B demands. Regulation B, which implements the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, requires that an adverse action notice state the creditor’s actual principal reasons for the decision, not generic categories or factors that merely influenced a score somewhere in the process. A factor-ranked output is evidence of compliance only if a defined sampling protocol shows that the ranked factors match those actual reasons. The FL-014 row as recorded specifies neither the sampling frame nor the comparison method, so its status is unverified rather than met (checked 2026-09-13).

FL-015 fails for a different reason. A readability score measures whether text is written at an appropriate grade level. It does not show whether an actual layperson understood the text. Readability stands in for comprehension. A matrix that lets it substitute for the comprehension evidence the requirement calls for has confused what was tested with what was required. A control sometimes checks something narrower than its requirement. Chapter 7 established that the gap between them must be recorded, and not folded into a plain “met” status that says nothing about the gap. The traceability matrix is where that recording happens. An organization reading only the control column would believe both requirements satisfied. Only the evidence column, read against what each requirement demands, reveals that neither one is.

Table 15.1’s status column is deliberately cautious. It marks a requirement met only where the evidence plainly supports it. A requirement’s status feeds the risk decisions described in Chapter 14. An unverified requirement cannot support a residual risk acceptance the way a fully verified one can. A residual risk acceptance record should name the specific matrix entries it relies on. Then a later change can trigger the reassessment that Chapter 14 already requires. Without that link, the change sits unnoticed in a matrix nobody revisits. The trigger might be a change to a requirement’s status, a control that stops working, or a sampling protocol that proves inadequate.

Table 15.1 shows only the columns needed here. A matrix for a live system usually tracks more. It records the system and data version each row applies to. It records the assessor and the verification date. Where a gap has been formally accepted and not only recorded, it records an exception and its expiry. Expiry and reassessment live in that residual-risk record. They are not duplicated here.

15.3 Model cards

A model card records, section by section, what a model is for and how it performed. Five sections are the minimum, and they are intended use, training data, performance and evaluation results, limitations, and maintenance information. The original model card framework covers a broader set of categories, among them intended users, factors, ethical considerations, and quantitative analyzes broken out by subgroup rather than averaged together. A deploying organization should add whichever of those categories a specific system’s risk profile calls for instead of treating this five-category core as exhaustive. A model card is not the only document that this evidence standard reaches, because it describes one model. A system built from several models, or one operating under a specific legal regulation, typically needs a broader system card covering the whole system rather than a single model, or the fuller technical documentation that a regulation like the EU AI Act requires. These documents come on top of the model card, not instead of it. Each section exists to answer a specific question that a deploying organization needs answered before relying on the model. A card missing a section has left that question unanswered rather than implicitly answered favorably.

Reading a model card adversarially means reading it as a skeptical reviewer would. The reviewer treats what the card omits or leaves vague as an unresolved evidence request. An omission is neither a neutral gap nor a settled fact about what happened. A card with no limitations section does not reveal why the section is missing. Limitations testing might not have been done. It might have been done and not published. Or it might have been left out by an author who did not think to include it. A reviewer cannot tell which from the card alone. What the reviewer can do is ask directly. Was limitations testing performed? If so, can the protocol and the disaggregated results be produced?

A vague limitations section raises the same kind of question. An example is a section stating that the model “may perform differently across subgroups” without naming which subgroups or by how much. Vagueness settles nothing. It does not by itself establish that unfavorable testing occurred and was withheld. Treating vagueness as proof of a hidden bad result substitutes suspicion for evidence, the same substitution an unsourced number makes when it stands in for justification. The useful move is the same in both cases. A reviewer should request the protocol and the disaggregated results and compare the evaluation population against the intended deployment population. If the answer is a refusal, or the material is unavailable, the reviewer should record that as its own finding rather than infer what it means.

A thin performance section prompts its own follow-up. Suppose it reports only an aggregate accuracy figure with no breakdown by group. The question is the one that Chapter 6 applies at selection. What is the performance broken out by the specific populations this deployment will serve? An aggregate figure can hide a disparity, just as a fairness metric chosen without scrutiny can.

Table 15.2. Model card sections and the question each one should answer

Section What it should establish Question to ask when it is missing or vague
Intended use What the model is validated to do, and what it is not validated for What specific uses were tested, and what happens if this deployment falls outside them
Training data What the model learned from and what populations that data represents Does the training population match the deployment population, and if not, in what way
Performance and evaluation How the model performed, against what benchmark, disaggregated by relevant subgroup Can the disaggregated results be produced, not only the aggregate figure
Limitations Known failure modes and the conditions under which performance degrades Was limitations testing performed, and can the protocol and results be produced
Provenance and version Which model version the card describes and how it relates to earlier versions Does this card match the version actually deployed, and what changed since the last version
Maintenance and ownership Who owns the card, how to report an issue, and when it was last reviewed Who is the current owner, and when was the card last checked against the current model
Security, misuse, and legal restrictions Known misuse risks and any legal or licensing restriction on use Has a misuse review been performed, and what does the license actually permit
Deployment dependencies and monitoring What the model assumes about its runtime environment, and how it is monitored once deployed What happens to the card’s claims if a dependency changes, and who is watching for that

Table 15.2 is a checklist to run against any model card. It applies whether the card has the five-category core or the fuller original framework. A section that looks thorough still gets its question, because the point of reading adversarially is asking the question, not assuming a complete-looking section has already answered it.

15.4 Audit trails and logging

A decision may need to be reconstructed months later. An affected individual may challenge it. A regulator or an internal review may ask about it. So the organization must capture information at the time of the decision that nobody expects to need until they suddenly do. Table 15.3 lists the five items a log needs at minimum and the further items that reconstruction may need depending on the system and the regulation it operates under, among them the authorization decision that section 15.5 develops for agentic systems.

Table 15.3. Audit log items, minimum and conditional

Item What it records When needed
Input The input the system received Minimum
Model and configuration version Which model version processed the input, and the configuration, the set of settings the model ran with Minimum
Output The output produced Minimum
Human override Any override, meaning a person changing what the system decided, and the reason given for it Minimum
Timestamp When each of the other items occurred Minimum
Policy or tool version The policy or tool version in force at the time Where reconstruction needs it
Reviewer or approver Who reviewed or approved the decision Where reconstruction needs it
Error or exception record Where the system failed to complete normally Where reconstruction needs it
Correlation identifier A shared label that ties together the related entries of a request spanning multiple systems or a multi-step agentic action Where reconstruction needs it
Authorization decision The authorization decision behind an action Where reconstruction needs it
Integrity record That the log itself has not been altered after the fact Where reconstruction needs it

A log missing the model version cannot show which version’s known limitations applied to a specific decision. That matters when a limitation is discovered after the fact. The organization then needs to know which past decisions the limitation might have affected.

Capturing “the input” does not mean storing it in full regardless of what it contains. Some inputs carry personal, legally protected, or otherwise sensitive material. For such inputs, the logging design should record a masked, hashed, or reference form that is enough to support reconstruction. The design should not default to full-content capture as the only way to meet the requirement. Access to any unmasked original should itself be controlled and separately logged.

Retention of this logging sits in real tension with privacy obligations. The same records that let an organization reconstruct a decision months later often contain personal data. Privacy regulation pushes organizations to minimize and delete that data promptly. The tension does not resolve to a single rule of thumb. It resolves to a retention schedule. The schedule comes from the specific law a system falls under and from the specific purpose each category of record serves.

The EU AI Act sets a concrete floor for a high-risk system once its high-risk obligations take effect. The Act does not leave retention for each organization to decide. Under the amended timetable, Annex III and Annex I cover different sets of high-risk uses and take effect on different dates. The Annex III high-risk rules apply from 2 December 2027, and the Annex I product-related high-risk rules apply from 2 August 2028. The Act assigns different duties to the provider that supplies the system and the deployer that uses it, and the documentation duty falls on the provider. From the applicable date, a provider must retain the automatically generated logs under its control for at least six months, and longer where other applicable Union or national law requires it. A deployer carries a separate retention duty for the logs under its own control. A provider separately must retain the system’s technical documentation for ten years after the system is placed on the market or put into service (Articles 18, 19, and 26, checked 2026-09-13).

A privacy regulation such as the GDPR points the other way. It favors keeping personal data no longer than the purpose for retaining it requires (Article 5). An organization under both obligations needs a schedule that treats operational logs, audit evidence, and technical documentation as separate categories, each with its own minimum and its own justification, instead of one plausibility test applied uniformly to everything the system produces.

15.5 Reconstructing agentic action: sequence and authority

Agentic systems make the reconstruction problem harder, because two things must be reconstructed and they are different things. The first is the action sequence. It records everything the agent could see when it acted, including its inputs, what it had looked up, and its prior tool results. The sequence also records the action the agent selected and which tool or service it called. A decision log captures the same kind of information for a predictive or generative system, and for an agentic system that information extends to a chain of actions instead of a single output.

The second is the authority chain. A sequence log does not automatically capture it. The authority chain records what authorization the agent acted under. That authorization is a decision more than a single fact. Reconstructing it means recording the elements the decision depended on, among them the credential issued to the agent and the scope that credential carried. Table 15.4 lists them.

Table 15.4. Elements of the authority chain

Element What it records
Principal Which principal requested the task. A principal is the party that asks the agent to act, and it can be a person or a software service
Policy What policy governed the decision to grant or deny the task
Scoped credential What credential was issued. A scoped credential is a digital pass that allows only a defined set of actions, and its scope is that set of actions
Registered agent identity Which identity the credential was issued to. A registered agent identity is the name under which the organization recorded the agent, and it was established under Chapter 12’s registration requirement
Constraints and validity window The credential’s constraints and its validity window, the period during which it works
Delegation Whether a supervising agent delegated the task to a subordinate one under a narrower scope than the one it held itself

How that credential evidence is represented depends on the architecture. A policy-enforcement system checks each request against the organization’s rules, and it can record the decision itself at the point where enforcement happens. The record holds who asked, what they asked to reach, and what they asked to do with it. It also holds the surrounding circumstances, which version of the rules applied, and what was allowed or refused. It can hold all of that with no separate credential object at all. A system built on OAuth-style token exchange instead records an issued token, its scope, and its validity. When one party passes the task to the next, a fresh credential at that hop is required only where the architecture’s own policy calls for one, not as a matter of general rule.

The chain must record the scope in force at the moment of the action, not at the moment the agent was registered. Scope can be narrowed, widened, or suspended after registration (Chapter 12). A log that checks only the registration-time scope will misstate what the agent was authorized to do when it acted.

A sequence log with a complete authority chain answers one question. It shows who was authorized to act and under what scope. It does not by itself answer who is accountable for the outcome. Whether the action was permitted is the authorization question. How much a given party brought the outcome about and who has to answer for it are different questions. An agent’s registered identity answers the authorization question in technical terms. It is not a person or a legal entity capable of answering for the outcome.

Establishing accountability means mapping the technical chain back to the human and organizational roles that Chapter 13 defines. The chain’s parts are the principal, the delegation, the credential, and the action. The mapping answers three questions. Who owned the decision to deploy the agent with this scope? Who is the designated risk owner if the action causes harm? Who had the authority to prevent it? A complete authority chain is necessary evidence for that mapping. It is not the mapping itself. Treating a fully recorded chain as proof of who is answerable skips the step that connects a technical record to an accountability decision.

Multi-agent architectures make the distinction between sequence and authority acute. The chain from a human principal to the agent that executes an action can run through several intermediate hops, a supervising agent delegating to a subordinate one that delegates again. Each hop must be recorded independently. The record includes whatever credential, token, or policy record the architecture issued or checked at that hop. It also includes the hop’s scope and validity window. A hop missing that record breaks the chain at that point, leaving an unanswerable gap between the principal who authorized the work in general terms and the specific agent that took the specific action under challenge.

Chapter 12’s registration requirement creates an identity that a log can reference. The authority chain records that identity in use at the moment of a specific action. It records the policy decision and the credential, which were verified and not just asserted. Preventive enforcement is what stops an out-of-scope action before it happens. It consists of the policy decision and a second check at the resource the agent calls when the action is attempted. Preventive enforcement does not itself depend on the log. Monitoring (Chapter 9) is the separate, ongoing check that compares what already happened against what was authorized. It can do that checking only if the data already exists in the log it monitors. A monitoring function built to compare actions against permission scope needs that data. It has nothing to compare against if the log never captured the policy decision and the scope in force at the moment of each action. The log design therefore decides whether that monitoring, and the accountability mapping that Chapter 13 requires, can run at all.

Read Figure 15.3’s two paths side by side instead of reasoning about a missing hop in the abstract. On the broken path, the principal and the acting agent have complete records. The supervising agent’s delegation step has a gap. The chain stops being followable at that gap. For reconstruction purposes, the chain is broken however complete the two recorded ends look. Even the complete path answers only the first of the figure’s two questions, who was authorized. The accountability step at the end of that path names who is answerable. Skipping that step, not a missing hop, is the more common failure in practice.

15.6 Evidence for regulators

Demonstrable compliance differs from believing that an organization complies. Northfield found this out when its confidence in MedAssist’s validated sensitivity produced no record a regulator would accept. Demonstrable compliance means a specific document, a specific test result, or a specific log entry exists and can be produced on request. It is tied to the specific requirement and the specific time period a regulator is asking about. Believing you comply means the organization’s staff are confident the underlying work was done. That confidence may well be true. Northfield was in that position, correct in substance but unable to prove it.

Three separate questions are involved, and keeping them separate matters.

  1. Did the system satisfy the substantive requirement?
  2. Did the organization create and retain the evidence that a specific law or policy requires it to keep?
  3. Is the evidence that exists sufficient for the regulator or decision-maker asking about it?

Some regulations treat the second question as its own independent obligation. The EU AI Act’s documentation and logging duties, described in section 15.4, are examples. Under such a regulation, a missing record can itself be a violation, however the first question would have come out. A missing record can also foreclose the organization’s ability to answer the third question favorably, even when the underlying practice was sound. The traceability matrix, the model card, and the logging are part of governance itself, not paperwork layered on top of it. They let an organization answer all three questions instead of only the first one. Northfield’s experience shows what happens when only the first question has a good answer.

15.7 Automating documentation and versioning governance artifacts

Documentation generated automatically from development artifacts that already exist, among them a model’s training configuration, its test suite results, and its deployment manifest, can be more reliable than documentation written separately after the fact, but only under three conditions.

  1. The source data feeding the generator has to be correct.
  2. The mapping from source to document has to be validated.
  3. The generator itself has to be versioned and reproducible.

A person still has to sign off that the generated section is complete and means what it appears to mean. Automation without those conditions does not remove error. It changes what kind of error occurs and how far it spreads. A faulty generator or a stale mapping can spread the same wrong number into every document it produces, at a scale a single transcription error never reaches. Consider a model card’s performance section. Generating it directly from the test suite’s output, rather than having someone transcribe the numbers into a document later, removes the error the Northfield example illustrates, a record that no longer matches what was tested. The fix works only if the pipeline generating that section is under the same change control and review as every other governance artifact.

Governance artifacts are maintained objects, not one-time deliverables. They need ongoing upkeep. A requirements template ages as the systems it is applied to change. A template last revised before agentic systems existed will produce weak requirements for agentic risk, in the way section 15.1’s testability standard warns against. This is not because anyone wrote a weak requirement, but because the template never prompted for a criterion tied to the limits of what an agent may do. A risk taxonomy ages the same way. A threshold set once, with no defined review cycle, ages the most invisibly of all. A threshold never announces that the conditions it was calibrated under no longer hold. Each of these needs a named owner and a scheduled review. Chapter 14’s reassessment triggers apply the same discipline to an individual system’s assessment. Here it applies to the governance program’s own tools, not to any single system the tools are used to review.

Updating a template, a taxonomy, or a threshold does not by itself update the systems already governed under the version being replaced. A governance program needs a migration step alongside the update. The step should identify which live systems the change affects and tell their owners. Without that step, a revised tool sits next to assessments and requirements that were never brought forward to match it.

Case in focus: one requirement, three paths to evidence

MEDASSIST · FAIRLEND · TALENTSCREEN · Hypothetical, following the running cases

A related transparency goal runs through all three running cases, but the concrete legal requirement differs in each domain. Credit and medical alerting both give the affected person, or the clinician acting on their behalf, a route to a specific explanation of the individual outcome. Employment works differently. TalentScreen is the applicant-screening tool that Calloway purchased. Its governing rule, New York City’s Local Law 144, requires a bias audit and advance notice before the tool is used. The rule does not require an individualized explanation to a rejected applicant. Tracing the transparency goal across all three domains shows how differently it gets satisfied, and through what different mechanisms. The mechanism depends on whether the organization built the system itself or purchased it, and this book calls those the build path and the buy path.

At Northfield, MedAssist generates its sepsis alert internally. The explanation requirement has direct evidence behind it, and Northfield controls that evidence end to end. Northfield logs how much each input pushed the alert up or down at alert time, and a clinician-facing summary is generated from those feature contributions. Northfield’s own validation team tested the summary for comprehensibility against a sample of nursing staff before deployment. The traceability matrix entry for this requirement cites Northfield’s own test data. If a regulator asks for the underlying evidence, Northfield can produce it directly. Northfield built the pipeline the evidence came from.

At Meridian, FAIRLEND’s adverse action explanation is also built internally. It follows the factor-ranked output that Regulation B’s reasons requirement calls for, as traced in section 15.2. The evidence chain looks similar to MedAssist’s in structure. It is internal test data, internally controlled and directly producible. The complication lies in the evidence’s adequacy, not its source. Section 15.2 showed that the evidence for FL-014 and FL-015 falls short of what each requirement demands. The matrix records that gap explicitly. It does not resolve it.

At Calloway, TalentScreen’s explanation to a rejected applicant is a requirement that Calloway’s own governance program has adopted. Local Law 144 itself does not compel it. Calloway also did not build the capability. It is a feature of the purchased tool. The evidence for whether it satisfies that internal requirement is whatever the vendor is willing to supply. Calloway has no visibility into TalentScreen’s internal architecture, and neither Northfield’s feature-contribution log nor Meridian’s factor ranking is a record of a model’s internal reasoning either. Both are outputs the model produces alongside its decision. They are useful evidence, but not a window into how the model arrived at the decision. What Calloway lacks, and Northfield and Meridian have, is any output of that kind to evaluate directly. The evidence available instead is vendor-supplied documentation that describes the explanation feature in general terms.

Reading that vendor documentation adversarially is one path to better evidence. It applies the approach of section 15.3 to a vendor artifact instead of a model card. It is not the only path available to a purchasing organization. Calloway can test the explanation feature’s outputs directly against known cases, without needing architectural visibility. It can negotiate audit and data-access rights into the vendor contract. It can commission an independent evaluation. It can validate a sample of explanations with actual rejected applicants, instead of assuming the vendor’s general description covers comprehensibility. It can track complaint volume against the feature over time.

None of these closes the evidence gap entirely. Pressing a reluctant vendor for stronger evidence remains necessary regardless, and that is Chapter 16’s subject. Still, a purchasing organization is not limited to accepting whatever the vendor is willing to supply.

Summary

A testable requirement’s statement needs five elements. These are an actor, a required behavior, operating conditions and population, a measurable criterion, and a verification method. The requirement’s record also carries an identifier, source, priority, owner, and status. These fields serve management, not testability. A weak requirement differs from a strong one in whether it names a specific, sourced threshold and population. The strong one is precise enough to fail a test.

A traceability matrix connects each requirement to its control and its evidence. Its evidence column, not its control column, reveals whether a requirement is met, unverified, or only addressed on paper. Any gap that Chapter 7’s testing showed must be recorded explicitly and not folded into a plain pass. A requirement’s status then feeds directly into the residual risk decisions that Chapter 14 describes.

A model card’s sections each answer a specific question. Reading a card adversarially treats an omission or a vague section as an unresolved evidence request that generates a follow-up question. It does not treat the gap as proof of what caused it.

An audit trail must capture, at minimum, the input, the model and configuration version, the output, any override, and a timestamp. These items support reconstruction months later. The specific system and regulation require additional fields. The law that governs each category of record sets its retention. One example is the EU AI Act’s six-month logging floor and ten-year documentation duty for covered systems. A single plausibility test applied to everything alike does not set retention.

Agentic systems require reconstructing two distinct things. The action sequence shows what happened. The authority chain shows what was authorized. That includes the policy decision, the credential, and the scope in force at the moment of action, not at registration. A multi-agent delegation chain breaks at any hop that is not independently recorded. A complete authority chain establishes who was authorized to act. It does not by itself establish who is accountable for the outcome. Accountability requires mapping the chain back to the human and organizational roles that Chapter 13 defines.

Demonstrable compliance means having created the evidence that a specific law or policy requires. It is a separate question from both substantive compliance and believing you comply. A regulator’s inquiry tests whether the organization keeps these three questions distinct.

Documentation generated automatically from development artifacts can be more reliable than documentation written separately after the fact. That holds only when the source data, the mapping, and the generator itself are under the same review as every other governance artifact. Governance artifacts also age. Templates, taxonomies, and thresholds need a named owner and a review cycle. Chapter 14 applies the same discipline to an individual system’s assessment, and here it applies to the program’s own tools.
