15. Documentation and Evidence

Consider a constructed scenario, continuing the running cases. When a regulator asked Northfield Health to demonstrate that MedAssist’s sepsis alert had performed within its validated bounds for the community hospital where a delayed alert contributed to Chapter 10’s incident, Northfield’s governance team went looking for the record. What they found was that the requirement the model had been built against, “the model shall flag deteriorating patients with clinically acceptable sensitivity,” had no acceptance criteria attached to it at all. Everyone who had worked on MedAssist believed the requirement had been met, because the model had performed well in the testing everyone remembered. No one could produce the record showing what “acceptable” meant, what threshold had been tested against it, or when that test had last been rerun for the community hospital population specifically. The belief was probably correct. It was also, for the regulator’s purposes, unusable. A regulator asking to see evidence cannot act on an organization’s confidence in its own memory; it can only act on what the organization can produce. Where a regulation requires the record to exist independently of the underlying practice, a missing record is its own separate problem, regardless of whether the practice itself was sound.

This chapter is about the documentation discipline that turns confidence into evidence. That discipline includes requirements specific enough to test and a traceability matrix connecting each requirement to what actually verifies it. It also includes a model card read the way a skeptical reviewer reads one, not the way its author intended it, and logging sufficient to reconstruct a decision or an action months after the fact. The harder version of that last problem, one agentic systems create, is also part of that discipline. Reconstructing what happened is not the same task as reconstructing who is answerable for it.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a requirement specific enough to test, and distinguish a weak version of a requirement from a strong one on sight
  • Build a traceability matrix connecting a requirement to its control and its evidence, and use it to answer whether a requirement is actually satisfied instead of merely addressed
  • Read a model card critically, treating an omission or a vague section as an unresolved evidence question to raise, not an incidental gap or a settled fact about what happened
  • Specify logging sufficient to reconstruct a decision or an agentic action, including the authority chain a decision log alone cannot supply, and distinguish that chain from the separate question of who is legally and organizationally accountable

15.1 Writing testable requirements

A testable requirement’s normative statement needs five elements to be verifiable, not aspirational. Those elements are the actor or system the requirement binds, the behavior required of it, the operating conditions and population the requirement applies to, a measurable criterion, and a method for verifying that criterion. The requirement’s record carries additional fields that matter for managing it rather than for testing it. A stable identifier lets it be referenced and traced, a source records where the requirement came from, and a priority indicates what happens when it competes with another requirement for resources. An owner and a status round out the record, tracking whether the requirement is currently met, partially met, unverified, or not applicable. A requirement that will be reused or revised also carries a version and the dependencies it has on other requirements. Northfield’s sensitivity requirement had no measurable criterion and no verification method. That is not a weaker version of a complete requirement. It is a statement of intent with no way to tell whether the intent was met.

Measurability alone does not make a requirement sound. A number attached to a requirement is only as good as the evidence that produced it. A clinical threshold, a testing cadence, or a population boundary needs justification from clinical evidence, applicable regulation, and the system’s own validation record, not from how precise it sounds on the page. The testability standard is easiest to teach by contrast. A weak version of Northfield’s requirement, “the model shall flag deteriorating patients with clinically acceptable sensitivity,” sounds specific because it names a metric, sensitivity, but it names no threshold and no population. Two reviewers applying the same words in good faith could therefore reach different conclusions about whether a given result satisfies it. Nothing in the requirement itself resolves the disagreement. “Clinically acceptable” carries real meaning to a clinician, but that meaning was never turned into something a test can check against. A strong version states the threshold and the population together and traces each number to where it came from. For example, it reads, “the model shall achieve sensitivity of at least [the threshold established in the device’s own validation record] for sepsis onset within [the detection window that record specifies], measured per hospital site, retested on [the cadence the record’s monitoring plan requires]”. The bracketed form here is deliberate. A real requirement fills each bracket from a specific clinical or regulatory source and records that source in the requirement’s evidence field. A requirement that instead states a number with no traceable origin has only replaced one unverifiable claim with a more convincing-looking one. A single sensitivity threshold is also not the whole of what a real acceptance decision would need. A clinical alert can hit a sensitivity target while still producing an unusable false-positive burden, poor calibration, or a workflow effect that makes clinicians tune it out. A complete requirement pairs sensitivity with the complementary measures the specific decision calls for, specificity, positive predictive value, calibration, or alert burden among them, instead of resting on one metric alone. What makes the strong version strong is that it can fail a test against a stated, sourced criterion, not that it contains numbers. The weak version cannot fail, because nothing was specified precisely enough to fail against. A requirement that cannot fail a test has not actually constrained anything.

15.2 Traceability

A traceability matrix connects a requirement to the control that addresses it and the evidence demonstrating the control works, and its purpose is answering three questions a requirements list alone cannot answer. Is this requirement addressed by anything? What specifically addresses it? And has that thing actually been verified to work, by whom, and as of when? Table 15.1 shows what a matrix with real rows looks like for two of FAIRLEND’s requirements.

Table 15.1. Traceability matrix for two FAIRLEND requirements

ID Requirement Control Evidence Status
FL-014 Adverse action notice must state the creditor’s actual principal reasons for a denial, per Regulation B, 12 CFR 1002.9 Explanation module generates factor-ranked output at denial time Sample of 50 denial explanations reviewed in Q2 2026; no sampling protocol, tolerance, or comparison method against the model’s actual decision logic was recorded Unverified. Evidence exists but does not establish that the stated factors are the actual principal reasons
FL-015 Explanation must be understandable to a layperson, not merely technically accurate Explanation module output reviewed against a plain-language readability standard Readability scoring on the same 50-sample set; no comprehension or usability testing with actual readers was performed Unverified. A readability score measures grade level, not whether a reader actually understood the explanation

Neither requirement can be marked simply “met” from the evidence recorded, and the reason differs for each. Regulation B, which implements the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, requires that an adverse action notice state the creditor’s actual principal reasons for the decision, not generic categories or factors that merely influenced a score somewhere in the process. A factor-ranked output is evidence of compliance only if a defined sampling protocol establishes that the ranked factors match those actual reasons. The FL-014 row as recorded specifies neither the sampling frame nor the comparison method, so its status is unverified rather than met (checked 2026-09-13). FL-015 fails for a different reason. A readability score measures whether text is written at an appropriate grade level, not whether an actual layperson reader understood it. A matrix that lets a proxy measure like readability substitute for the comprehension evidence the requirement actually calls for has confused what was tested with what was required. Chapter 7 established that a control verifying something narrower than its own requirement must have that gap recorded rather than absorbed into an unqualified “met” status, and the traceability matrix is where that recording actually happens. An organization reading only the control column would believe both requirements satisfied, and only the evidence column, read against what the requirement actually demands, reveals that neither one is.

Table 15.1’s status column is deliberately conservative, and that conservatism connects forward to Chapter 14. The status a requirement carries here is an input to the risk decisions Chapter 14 describes, since an unverified requirement cannot support a residual risk acceptance the way a fully verified one can. A residual risk acceptance record under Chapter 14’s own standard should name the specific matrix entries it is relying on. That way, a later change can trigger the reassessment Chapter 14 already requires instead of sitting unnoticed in a matrix nobody revisits. The trigger might be a change to a requirement’s status, a control that stops working, or a sampling protocol that turns out to have been inadequate. Table 15.1 keeps to the columns this chapter’s teaching point needs. A production matrix for a live system typically also tracks the system and data version each row applies to, the assessor and verification date, and an exception and expiry where a gap has been formally accepted, not simply recorded. Expiry and reassessment specifically live in the residual-risk record Chapter 14 already requires, instead of being duplicated here.

15.3 Model cards

A model card, at minimum, should cover intended use, stating what the model is meant to do and, as important, what it is not validated for. It should also cover training data, describing what the model learned from and what populations that data represents. Performance and evaluation results should report how the model performed and against what benchmark, and limitations should name known failure modes and the conditions under which performance degrades. Maintenance information should state who owns the card, how to report an issue, and when it was last updated. The original model card framework covers a broader set of categories still, including intended users, factors, ethical considerations, and quantitative analyses disaggregated by subgroup. A deploying organization should add whichever of those a specific system’s risk profile calls for, instead of treating this five-category core as exhaustive. A model card is also not the only document this chapter’s evidence standard reaches; it describes one model. A system built from several models, or a system operating under a specific legal regulation, typically needs a broader system card or the fuller technical documentation a regulation like the EU AI Act requires, on top of, not instead of, the model card itself. Each section exists to answer a specific question a deploying organization needs answered before relying on the model, and a card missing a section has left that question unanswered rather than implicitly answered favorably.

Reading a model card adversarially means treating what it omits or leaves vague as an unresolved evidence request, not a neutral gap or a settled fact about what happened. A card with no limitations section does not reveal why the section is missing. Limitations testing might not have been done. It might have been done and not published. Or it might simply have been left out by an author who did not think to include it. A reviewer cannot tell which from the card alone. What the reviewer can do is ask directly. Was limitations testing performed, and if so, can the protocol and the disaggregated results be produced? A vague limitations section, one stating that the model “may perform differently across subgroups” without naming which subgroups or by how much, raises the same kind of question rather than settling it. It does not by itself establish that unfavorable testing occurred and was withheld, and treating vagueness as proof of a hidden bad result substitutes suspicion for evidence in exactly the way this chapter’s own standard rejects elsewhere. The useful move is the same in both cases. A reviewer should request the protocol and the disaggregated results, compare the evaluation population against the intended deployment population, and record a refusal or an unavailability as its own finding rather than inferring what it means. A thin performance section reporting only an aggregate accuracy figure with no disaggregation generates the same direct follow-up question Chapter 6 already applies at selection. What is the performance broken out by the specific populations this deployment will actually serve? An aggregate figure can hide a disparity as easily as a fairness metric chosen without scrutiny can.

Table 15.2. Model card sections and the question each one should answer

Section What it should establish Question to ask when it is missing or vague
Intended use What the model is validated to do, and what it is not validated for What specific uses were tested, and what happens if this deployment falls outside them
Training data What the model learned from and what populations that data represents Does the training population match the deployment population, and if not, in what way
Performance and evaluation How the model performed, against what benchmark, disaggregated by relevant subgroup Can the disaggregated results be produced, not only the aggregate figure
Limitations Known failure modes and the conditions under which performance degrades Was limitations testing performed, and can the protocol and results be produced
Provenance and version Which model version the card describes and how it relates to earlier versions Does this card match the version actually deployed, and what changed since the last version
Maintenance and ownership Who owns the card, how to report an issue, and when it was last reviewed Who is the current owner, and when was the card last checked against the current model
Security, misuse, and legal restrictions Known misuse risks and any legal or licensing restriction on use Has a misuse review been performed, and what does the license actually permit
Deployment dependencies and monitoring What the model assumes about its runtime environment, and how it is monitored once deployed What happens to the card’s claims if a dependency changes, and who is watching for that

Table 15.2 is a checklist to run against any model card regardless of how complete it looks, whether the card ships with the five-category core or the fuller original framework. A section that appears thorough still gets its question, because the point of reading adversarially is asking the question, not assuming a complete-looking section has already answered it.

15.4 Audit trails and logging

A decision may need to be reconstructed months later, under a challenge from an affected individual, a regulator, or an internal review. That means capturing information at the time of the decision that nobody expects to need until they suddenly do. At minimum, that means capturing the input the system received, the model and configuration version that processed it, the output produced, any human override and the reason given for it, and a timestamp establishing exactly when each of these occurred. Depending on the system and the regulation it operates under, reconstruction may also require the policy or tool version in force at the time, who reviewed or approved the decision, and an error or exception record where the system failed to complete normally. It may also require a correlation identifier tying together the related entries of a request that spans multiple systems or a multi-step agentic action, and the authorization decision behind an action (section 15.5 develops this for agentic systems specifically). It may also require an integrity record establishing that the log itself has not been altered after the fact. A log missing the model version cannot show which version’s known limitations applied to a specific decision. That matters when a limitation is discovered after the fact and an organization needs to know which past decisions it might have affected. Capturing “the input” does not mean storing it in full regardless of what it contains. Where an input carries personal, privileged, or otherwise sensitive material, the logging design should record a masked, hashed, or reference form sufficient to support reconstruction, rather than defaulting to full-content capture as the only way to satisfy this requirement. Access to any unmasked original should itself be controlled and separately logged.

Retention of this logging sits in real tension with privacy obligations. The same records that let an organization reconstruct a decision months later often contain the personal data a privacy regulation pushes organizations to minimize and delete promptly. That tension does not resolve to a single rule of thumb; it resolves to a retention schedule set by the specific law a system falls under and the specific purpose each category of record serves. The EU AI Act, for a high-risk system once its high-risk obligations take effect, sets a concrete floor rather than leaving retention to organizational judgment. Under the amended timetable, the Annex III high-risk rules apply from 2 December 2027 and the Annex I product-related high-risk rules apply from 2 August 2028. From that date, a provider must retain the automatically generated logs under its control for at least six months, longer where other applicable Union or national law requires it. A deployer carries a separate retention duty for the logs under its own control. A provider separately must retain the system’s technical documentation for ten years after the system is placed on the market or put into service (Articles 18, 19, and 26, checked 2026-09-13). A privacy regulation such as the GDPR points the other way, toward keeping personal data no longer than the purpose for retaining it requires (Article 5). An organization operating under both obligations needs a schedule that treats operational logs, audit evidence, and technical documentation as separate categories with separate minimums and separate justifications, instead of one plausibility test applied uniformly to everything the system produces.

15.5 Reconstructing agentic action: sequence and authority

Agentic systems make the reconstruction problem much harder, because two things need to be reconstructable and they are not the same thing. The action sequence records the observable state available to the agent at the time, its inputs, retrieved context, and prior tool results, the action it selected, and what it actually invoked. That is the same category of information a decision log captures for a predictive or generative system, extended here to a chain of actions, not a single output. The authority chain records something a sequence log does not automatically capture at all. It captures what authorization the agent acted under. That authorization is not a single fact so much as a decision, and reconstructing it means recording the elements the decision actually depended on. Those elements start with which human or service principal requested the task and what policy governed the decision to grant or deny it. They also include what scoped credential was actually issued, to which registered agent identity established under Chapter 12’s registration requirement, and what constraints and validity window applied to that credential. Finally, they include whether a supervising agent delegated the task to a subordinate one under a narrower scope than the one it held itself. How that credential evidence is represented depends on the architecture. A policy-enforcement system can record the decision itself, subject, resource, action, context, policy version, and outcome, at the point enforcement happens, with no separate credential object at all. A system built on OAuth-style token exchange instead records an issued token, its scope, and its validity. A fresh credential at a given hop is required only where the architecture’s own policy calls for one, not as a matter of general rule. Critically, the chain has to record what scope was actually in force at the moment of the action, not at the moment the agent was originally registered. Chapter 12 established that scope can be narrowed, widened, or suspended after registration. A log that checks only the registration-time scope will misstate what the agent was actually authorized to do when it acted.

A sequence log with a complete authority chain still only answers who was authorized to act and under what scope; it does not by itself answer who is accountable for the outcome. Authorization, causal contribution, and legal or organizational accountability are different questions, and an agent’s registered identity is a technical answer to the first, not a person or a legal entity capable of answering the third. Establishing accountability means mapping the technical chain, principal, delegation, credential, action, back to the human and organizational roles Chapter 13 defines. That mapping identifies who owned the decision to deploy the agent with this scope, who is the designated risk owner if the action causes harm, and who had the authority to have prevented it. A complete authority chain is necessary evidence for that mapping. It is not the mapping itself, and treating a fully recorded chain as proof of who is answerable skips the step that actually connects a technical record to an accountability decision.

Multi-agent architectures make the sequence-versus-authority distinction acute rather than incidental. The chain from a human principal to the agent that executes an action can run through several intermediate hops, a supervising agent delegating to a subordinate one that delegates again. Each hop has to be independently recorded, including whatever credential, token, or policy record the architecture issued or checked at that hop, its scope, and its validity window. A hop missing that record breaks the chain at exactly that point, leaving an unanswerable gap between the principal who authorized the work in general terms and the specific agent that took the specific action under challenge.

Registration, which Chapter 12 required, creates the identity a log can reference. The authority chain this section specifies is what records that identity actually in use, at the moment of a specific action, under a policy decision and a credential that were actually verified, not simply asserted. Preventive enforcement, the policy decision and the runtime recheck at the resource the agent actually calls, is what stops an out-of-scope action before it happens, and it does not itself depend on the log. Chapter 9’s monitoring is the separate, ongoing check that compares what already happened against what was authorized, and it can only do that checking if this data already exists in the log it monitors. A monitoring function built to compare actions against permission scope therefore has nothing to compare against if the log was never designed to capture the policy decision and the scope in force at the moment of each action in the first place. Specifying the log design here is not a documentation nicety; it is what makes the monitoring Chapter 9 already assumes, and the accountability mapping Chapter 13 requires, actually possible to run.

Read Figure 15.3’s two paths side by side instead of reasoning about a missing hop in the abstract. The broken path shows concretely where a chain with a complete record at the principal and the acting agent, but a gap at the supervising agent’s delegation step, stops being followable. For reconstruction purposes, that gap makes the chain broken regardless of how complete the two recorded ends look. Even the complete path answers only the first of the figure’s two questions, who was authorized. The accountability step at the end of that path is what actually names who is answerable, and skipping it, not a missing hop, is the more common failure in practice.

15.6 Evidence for regulators

Demonstrable compliance is different from believing an organization complies, and the difference is exactly what Northfield discovered when its confidence in MedAssist’s validated sensitivity produced no record a regulator would accept. Demonstrable compliance means a specific document, a specific test result, or a specific log entry exists and can be produced on request, tied to the specific requirement and the specific time period a regulator is asking about. Believing you comply means the organization’s staff are confident the underlying work was done, which may well be true. That confidence, on its own, was exactly the position Northfield was in, correct in substance but unable to prove it. These are three separate questions, not one, and keeping them separate matters. Did the system actually satisfy the substantive requirement? Did the organization create and retain the evidence a specific law or policy requires it to keep? And is the evidence that exists sufficient for the regulator or decision-maker asking about it? Some regulations, including the EU AI Act’s documentation and logging duties described in section 15.4, treat the second question as its own independent obligation. A missing record can itself be a violation, regardless of how the first question would have come out. Such a gap can also foreclose the organization’s ability to answer the third question favorably, even when the underlying practice was sound. The traceability matrix, the model card, and the logging this chapter has described are not paperwork layered on top of governance. They are what lets an organization answer all three questions instead of only the first one, and Northfield’s experience shows exactly what happens when only the first question has a good answer.

15.7 Automating documentation and versioning governance artifacts

Documentation generated automatically from the development artifacts that already exist, a model’s training configuration, its test suite results, its deployment manifest, can be more reliable than documentation written separately after the fact, but only under specific conditions. The source data feeding the generator has to be correct, the mapping from source to document has to be validated, and the generator itself has to be versioned and reproducible. A person still has to sign off that the generated section is complete and means what it appears to mean. Automation without those conditions does not remove error. It changes what kind of error occurs and how far it spreads. A faulty generator or a stale mapping can spread the same wrong number into every document it produces, at a scale a single transcription error never reaches. Generating a model card’s performance section directly from the test suite’s actual output, rather than having someone transcribe the numbers into a document later, removes the specific error this chapter’s Northfield example illustrates, a record that no longer matches what was actually tested. That fix works only provided the pipeline generating that section is itself under the same change control and review this chapter asks of every other governance artifact.

Governance artifacts themselves are maintained objects rather than one-time deliverables, and this section closes on the discipline that follows from that. A requirements template ages as the systems it is applied to change. A template last revised before agentic systems existed will produce weak requirements for agentic risk, in exactly the way section 15.1’s testability standard warns against. This is not because anyone wrote a weak requirement, but because the template never prompted for a scope-specific acceptance criterion. A risk taxonomy ages the same way. A threshold set once, with no defined review cycle, ages the most invisibly of all, because a threshold never announces that the conditions it was calibrated under no longer hold. Each of these needs a named owner and a scheduled review, the same discipline Chapter 14’s reassessment triggers apply to an individual system’s assessment, applied here to the governance program’s own tools, not to any single system the tools are used to review. Updating a template, a taxonomy, or a threshold does not by itself update the systems already governed under the version being replaced. A governance program needs a migration step alongside the update itself. It should identify which live systems the change affects, and tell their owners. Otherwise a revised tool sits next to assessments and requirements that were never brought forward to match it.

Case in focus: one requirement, three paths to evidence

MEDASSIST · FAIRLEND · TALENTSCREEN · Hypothetical, following the running cases

A related transparency goal runs through all three running cases, but the concrete legal requirement is not the same in each domain. Credit and medical alerting both give the affected person, or the clinician acting on their behalf, a route to a specific explanation of the individual outcome. Employment does not work the same way. TalentScreen’s governing rule, New York City’s Local Law 144, requires a bias audit and advance notice before the tool is used, not an individualized explanation to a rejected applicant. Tracing the underlying transparency goal across all three domains still shows how differently, and through what different mechanisms, it gets satisfied, depending on whether the underlying system was built internally or purchased.

At Northfield, MedAssist generates its sepsis alert internally, and the explanation requirement has direct evidence behind it that Northfield controls end to end. The model’s feature contributions are logged at alert time, a clinician-facing summary is generated from those contributions, and Northfield’s own validation team tested the summary against a sample of nursing staff for comprehensibility before deployment. The traceability matrix entry for this requirement cites Northfield’s own test data, and if a regulator asks for the underlying evidence, Northfield can produce it directly, because Northfield built the pipeline the evidence came from.

At Meridian, FAIRLEND’s adverse action explanation is also built internally, following the Regulation-B-driven factor-ranked output section 15.2 traced, and the evidence chain looks similar to MedAssist’s in structure. It is internal test data, internally controlled and directly producible. The complication is not the evidence’s source but its adequacy, since section 15.2 already showed FL-014 and FL-015’s evidence falling short of what the requirement actually demands, a gap the matrix records explicitly rather than resolves.

At Calloway, TalentScreen’s explanation to a rejected applicant is a requirement Calloway’s own governance program has adopted, not one Local Law 144 itself compels. It is also not a capability Calloway built at all; it is a feature of the purchased tool, and the evidence for whether it satisfies that internal requirement is whatever the vendor is willing to supply. Calloway has no visibility into TalentScreen’s internal architecture, and neither Northfield’s feature-contribution log nor Meridian’s factor ranking is actually a record of a model’s internal reasoning either. Both are outputs the model produces alongside its decision, useful evidence but not a window into how the model arrived at it. What Calloway lacks that Northfield and Meridian have is any output like that at all to evaluate directly, and the evidence available instead is vendor-supplied documentation describing the explanation feature in general terms. Reading that vendor documentation adversarially, per section 15.3’s approach applied to a vendor artifact rather than a model card specifically, is one path to better evidence, but not the only one available to a purchasing organization. Calloway can also test the explanation feature’s outputs directly against known cases without needing architectural visibility, negotiate audit and data-access rights into the vendor contract, and commission an independent evaluation. It can also validate a sample of explanations with actual rejected applicants, instead of assuming the vendor’s general description covers comprehensibility, and track complaint volume against the feature over time. None of these closes the evidence gap entirely, and pressing a reluctant vendor for stronger evidence, which Chapter 16 covers in full, remains necessary regardless. Still, a purchasing organization is not limited to accepting whatever the vendor is willing to supply.

Summary

A testable requirement’s statement needs an actor, a required behavior, operating conditions and population, a measurable criterion, and a verification method. Its record additionally carries an identifier, source, priority, owner, and status for management, not testability. The difference between a weak and a strong requirement is whether it names a specific, sourced threshold and population precisely enough to fail a test. A traceability matrix connects each requirement to its control and its evidence. Its evidence column, not its control column, is what reveals whether a requirement is met, unverified, or merely addressed on paper, with any gap Chapter 7’s testing already showed recorded explicitly rather than absorbed into an unqualified pass. A requirement’s status then feeds directly into the residual risk decisions Chapter 14 describes.

A model card’s sections each answer a specific question, and reading one adversarially treats an omission or a vague section as an unresolved evidence request generating a follow-up question, not as proof of what caused the gap. Audit trails must capture at minimum the input, the model and configuration version, the output, any override, and a timestamp to support reconstruction months later. Additional fields are required by the specific system and regulation. Retention is set by the law each category of record falls under, such as the EU AI Act’s six-month logging floor and ten-year documentation duty for covered systems, not by a single plausibility test applied to everything alike. Agentic systems require reconstructing two distinct things. The action sequence shows what happened, and the authority chain shows what was authorized, by what policy decision, under what credential, and with what scope in force at the moment of action, not at registration. A multi-agent delegation chain breaks at any hop that is not independently recorded. A complete authority chain establishes who was authorized to act; it does not by itself establish who is accountable for the outcome, which requires mapping the chain back to the human and organizational roles Chapter 13 defines. Demonstrable compliance, having created the evidence a specific law or policy requires, is a separate question from both substantive compliance and from believing you comply. Keeping the three questions distinct is what a regulator’s inquiry actually tests. Documentation generated automatically from development artifacts can be more reliable than documentation written separately after the fact, but only when the source data, the mapping, and the generator itself are under the same review this chapter asks of every other governance artifact. Governance artifacts themselves, templates, taxonomies, and thresholds, age and require a named owner and a review cycle, the same discipline applied to the program’s own tools that Chapter 14 applies to an individual system’s assessment.

  2. Specify the fields an audit log needs at minimum to reconstruct a contested decision six months later, name one additional field a regulated agentic system might need beyond that minimum, and explain why the model version field matters independently of the output field.

SPOT THE RISK

  1. A multi-agent system’s log records the human principal’s initial authorization and the acting agent’s final action, but not the intermediate supervising agent’s delegation step.
