Appendix C: Templates and Checklists

These nine templates are offered under Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0 International (CC BY-NC 4.0) for readers to copy, adapt, and use directly in their own organizations. Reuse under that license has to satisfy its actual conditions, not a looser summary of them. The license requires attribution and a notice of changes made. Its NonCommercial term is defined by whether the use is primarily intended for or directed toward commercial advantage or monetary compensation, a broader test than resale alone. This paragraph is a summary for convenience, not a substitute for the license text itself, which a reader relying on these templates commercially or without attribution should read directly. Within those conditions, fill in the fields, cut what your organization does not need, add what it does, and use the result as a working document instead of treating this appendix as an illustration to reference from a distance. Each field below is cross-referenced to the chapter section that establishes it, so a filled-in template can be traced back to the specific requirement it satisfies.

Each template below is scoped to hold one record for one system, model, agent, or event; an organization with many systems keeps many filled instances of the same template instead of trying to make a single instance cover a portfolio. Fields marked as cross-references exist so the nine templates link into one connected set of records, rather than nine disconnected documents. That is what lets a later reviewer trace a specific system’s history across inventory, assessment, and incident records without reconstructing the connections from memory. Because a specific chapter’s model can itself be revised in a later edition, each template also carries the same minimal record-control header. That header records a template ID and version, the book edition and chapter section it was built from, and the date it was last checked against that section’s current text. That lets a later reviewer tell which version of a chapter’s model a specific filled instance was built against. Every filled instance should also carry its own status, author, reviewer, approver, effective date, and change history in addition to its substantive fields, following Chapter 15’s evidence-record discipline generally, not restating it individually in each template below.

1. AI Use Case Documentation

Complete before any development or procurement work begins, per Chapter 4’s argument that governance starting after a system exists is inspection rather than governance.

  • Use case name and one-sentence description
  • Business owner, naming a specific person and role, not a department alone, with the effective date this person took the role so the record stays traceable when the person changes
  • Problem statement, naming what decision or task this system addresses and for whom
  • Why an AI approach specifically, as distinct from a rules-based or manual alternative
  • Expected benefit, and what happens if this use case is not pursued at all
  • Proposed path: build, fine-tune, or buy (Chapter 6)
  • System class: predictive, generative, or agentic, or a mix (Chapter 3, section 3.1)
  • Affected population, naming who experiences this system’s outputs or actions
  • Decision type: recommendation to a human, or autonomous action (Chapter 3, section 3.6)
  • Date submitted and date entered into the system inventory below

2. System Inventory Record

One record per system, maintained per Chapter 3, section 3.3, as a living artifact with an owner and a review date, not a one-time exercise. A system discovered through the shadow AI discovery process in Chapter 3, section 3.4, uses this same record; note the discovery method in the last field rather than treating a discovered system as a different kind of record.

  • System name and internal identifier
  • Business owner and technical owner (may differ)
  • Business unit and physical or organizational location of use
  • System class: predictive, generative, or agentic
  • Deployment status: in development, piloting, in production, retired
  • Model or models used (cross-reference to Model Inventory Record entries)
  • Data sources and categories of personal data processed, if any
  • Risk classification and the date it was assigned (cross-reference to Impact Assessment)
  • Applicable regulatory mappings (Chapter 3, section 3.7; Chapter 18, section 18.3)
  • Date of last review and scheduled next review date
  • Record owner, last update date, and, if applicable, discovery method if this system was not originally self-reported

3. Model Inventory Record

One record per model, distinct from the System Inventory Record above because one model can serve several systems and one system can call several models, per Chapter 3’s system-versus-model distinction.

  • Model name and version identifier
  • Developer or source (internal build, fine-tune of a named base model, or purchased)
  • Path: build, fine-tune, adapter, or licensed third-party (Chapter 16, section 16.6)
  • Training data summary, or “not visible to this organization” where the path is buy (Chapter 5)
  • Intended use and explicitly out-of-scope uses
  • Known limitations and failure modes as currently understood
  • Systems this model serves (cross-reference to System Inventory Record entries)
  • Date last validated and validation method used
  • Reassessment triggers that would require re-validation (Chapter 14, section 14.5)

4. Model Card

For a model an organization builds or fine-tunes itself. For a purchased model, use this same structure to record what the vendor has and has not disclosed, per Chapter 15’s adversarial reading approach, marking undisclosed fields explicitly instead of leaving them blank.

  • Model name, version, and date
  • Intended use and explicitly out-of-scope uses
  • Training data description, covering sources, time period, and known composition, or the vendor’s disclosed description quoted directly if this organization cannot see the underlying data
  • Performance metrics, the population and methodology they were measured against, and the date measured
  • Fairness testing summary, covering metrics used, populations compared, and findings (Chapter 7)
  • Known limitations and documented failure modes
  • Safety and refusal behavior notes, where applicable (Chapter 11)
  • Retraining or update history and how a user of this card is notified of changes
  • Contact point for questions this card does not answer

5. Impact Assessment

Follow Chapter 14’s five-component structure as it stands in this book’s current text. That structure is itself the subject of ongoing refinement across this book’s own chapters, so record which edition and check date this template instance was built against rather than assuming the structure is fixed for all time. Complete before deployment, and again whenever a reassessment trigger fires. Where a stakeholder who would normally be consulted cannot be reached, record which of Chapter 14’s consultation substitutes was used instead and what it cannot recover compared to direct consultation.

  • System description, covering what it does, who it affects, and at what scale
  • Risk identification, listing each identified risk, its likelihood and severity (Chapter 14, section 14.3), and its source
  • Mitigation, naming the specific control addressing each risk and whether it reduces the risk’s occurrence or only detects it after the fact (Chapter 14, section 14.4)
  • Residual risk, stating what remains after mitigation explicitly instead of leaving it implied by silence
  • Documented decision, naming the accountable approver, the date, and the specific reasoning accepting the residual risk or requiring further mitigation before proceeding
  • Reassessment trigger conditions and next scheduled review date

6. Vendor Assessment

Structured across Chapter 16’s four assessment dimensions, built against this book’s current chapter text and, like template 5, worth re-checking against a later edition rather than assumed fixed. Log every question the vendor declined to answer in the final field; a declined question is itself evidence.

  • Provider dimension, covering company stability, support commitments, and track record
  • System dimension, covering what the model actually does, its documented limitations, and disaggregated performance data if obtainable
  • Documentation dimension, covering model card completeness, per template 4 above, and what is missing
  • Compliance dimension, covering applicable certifications, audit rights offered, and regulatory mappings the vendor supports
  • Contract provisions confirmed present, covering information rights, audit rights, change notification, incident cooperation, liability allocation, and exit terms (Chapter 16, section 16.5)
  • Questions the vendor declined to answer, and whether each decline is disqualifying (Chapter 16, section 16.3)
  • Overall recommendation and named approver

7. Agent Registration and Permission Scope

One record per agentic system or agent role, per Chapter 12’s argument that an agent’s authority must be recorded before it acts, not reconstructed afterward.

  • Agent name and the system it belongs to (cross-reference to System Inventory Record)
  • Issuing authority, naming who approved this agent’s credential and on what basis
  • Subject and audience, naming the actor the credential is issued to and the resource or party it is valid against
  • Scope of authority, naming the specific actions, resources, and purposes this agent is permitted to invoke without further human approval, and what is explicitly out of scope
  • Tools and systems this agent can access, and the permissions granted to each
  • Credential form and validity, describing how the scope is technically enforced (a scoped, time-bound credential per Chapter 15, section 15.5), its validity window, and its current revocation state
  • Policy decision point, naming what evaluates a given action against this scope at runtime and what happens on a policy denial
  • Escalation conditions, naming what triggers a required human-in-the-loop step and the approval and result recorded when one fires
  • Logging requirements, naming what is captured at each action, including the scope in force at the time of the action, a correlation ID linking related actions, and a tamper-evident integrity mark on the record (Chapter 15, section 15.5)
  • Monitoring, naming what ongoing check confirms this agent is not exceeding its recorded scope in practice and how an exception surfaces
  • Delegation chain, if this agent can invoke or supervise other agents, including each hop’s own scope and whether it can be narrower or wider than the delegating agent’s own
  • Review cadence and the date of last permission-scope review
  • Deregistration or scope-revocation process, who is authorized to invoke it, and what recovery or rollback applies to actions already taken under a credential later found to have been wrongly scoped

8. Incident Response

Structured per Chapter 10’s response sequence. Open a record at the moment an incident is identified, not after it is resolved.

  • Incident identifier, date and time first identified, and detection source (monitoring, user report, audit, vendor notice, or other)
  • Accountable owner for the response, named at the point the record is opened
  • Severity classification and the criteria that produced it
  • Systems and populations affected
  • Evidence preservation, naming what was captured before any remediation altered the system’s state and where it is retained
  • Containment actions taken, and the date and time each was taken
  • Assessment, stating what is currently known about scope and cause, updated as it changes, including root cause and any contributory factors distinct from it (Chapter 10’s investigation approach)
  • Communication log, naming who was notified, when, and with what content, kept current through resolution instead of written once at closure
  • Regulatory reporting determination, stating whether a notification obligation applies, under which specific regulation, in what role (for example, an EU AI Act operator’s serious-incident duty under Article 73), the applicable deadline measured from awareness, and the status of any required interim and final reports
  • Affected-party remedy, naming what correction, compensation, or other remedy was offered or provided, to whom, and when
  • Rollback or recovery, naming what was reverted or restored and confirming that the reversion actually took effect
  • Documentation of decisions made during the response, each with a timestamp and the name of who made it
  • Corrective action and its verification, naming the specific process or control change the incident produced and how its effectiveness was confirmed instead of only implemented
  • Closure criteria met, post-incident review date, and the lesson fed back into the relevant control, template, or training material
  • For a system with delayed or absent ground truth (Chapter 9), the method used to establish how many cases were actually affected, since a prevented or undetected outcome leaves no confirmed case to count directly

9. Governance Readiness

Per Chapter 17. Use this to conduct a current-state assessment, not to describe an aspirational future state.

  • Inventory assurance, meaning the percentage of known systems with a current inventory record, plus a separate estimate of unknown-system exposure from the date and method of the last discovery sweep (Chapter 3’s shadow AI discovery). A percentage of known systems alone cannot measure what the inventory has not yet found
  • Classification coverage, giving the percentage of inventoried systems with a current risk classification
  • Process versus practice, stating for each governance process that exists on paper whether it is actually used, for what proportion of applicable systems, and evidence supporting that answer (Chapter 17, section 17.2)
  • Requirements gathered, giving the date of last consultation with developers, business owners, and reviewers on where the process obstructs their work
  • Control proportionality, stating whether review depth is tied to risk tier or applied uniformly regardless of it
  • Cost model, stating whether each control’s cost has been estimated at production volume rather than pilot volume
  • Training completion, giving the percentage of each role trained and the date of last training update
  • Outcome metrics, covering issues found before deployment versus after, classification accuracy on independent review, and bypass rate by system and by team
  • Named owner of this readiness assessment and date completed
