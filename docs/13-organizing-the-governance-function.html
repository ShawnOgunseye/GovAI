13. Organizing the Governance Function

Consider a constructed scenario, continuing the running FAIRLEND case. Meridian Financial’s AI governance function existed, on paper, from the day its board approved its charter, a mandate to review AI systems for fairness, transparency, and risk before deployment, reporting to the chief risk officer, with responsibilities spanning every system class this book has covered. What the charter did not resolve, and what took the new function eighteen months to work out in practice, was what happened when its mandate intersected a model risk management function that had governed FAIRLEND’s underlying credit models for a decade. That function had its own validation standards, its own reporting line, and its own settled view of what “model governance” already meant before anyone used the words “AI governance” at Meridian at all.

This chapter is about the structural questions a charter alone does not answer. Those questions include which operating model an organization chooses and how that choice actually plays out across dozens of individual decision points, not just once on an org chart. They also include what roles and lines of defense a governance program needs to actually function instead of merely existing, and what happens, concretely, when a new AI governance function meets an incumbent with real authority already in place. A governance program without this structure has opinions; a governance program with it has authority, and the difference determines whether Meridian’s function could actually stop a deployment or only write a memo about one that proceeded anyway.

What you will be able to do

  • Compare centralized, federated, and hybrid operating models against organizational context and identify where the choice recurs across dozens of decisions instead of only once
  • Apply the three lines model to AI governance, naming which functions occupy which line
  • Design an acceptable use policy that combines a sanctioned alternative, technical controls, and monitoring instead of relying on a written prohibition alone
  • Navigate structural conflict with an incumbent function by locating where each function’s mandate actually starts and stops

13.1 Operating models

Centralized, federated, and hybrid arrangements are design patterns, not a performance ranking. Their suitability depends on the decision at issue, applicable law, organizational scale, risk concentration, local expertise, independence requirements, and the ability to aggregate evidence. These are factors current U.S. bank supervisory guidance on model risk management names explicitly as varying by organizational risk profile, size, complexity, and use instead of converging on one preferred structure. A centralized model concentrates governance authority in a single function that reviews and approves AI systems across the organization, producing consistency of standards and a single point of aggregate visibility. The cost is a bottleneck for an organization deploying AI faster than one central team can review it, and a central function that lacks the specific business context each deploying unit actually has. A federated model distributes governance authority to the business units deploying AI, each applying governance standards within its own domain, producing responsiveness and context-appropriate judgment, at the cost of the failure modes section 13.4 develops in detail. Those failure modes are standards drifting apart across units and nobody holding the aggregate picture regulators and boards actually need. A hybrid model splits authority by some other dimension entirely; section 13.3 develops one candidate split whose suitability, like the other two models, must be tested against an organization’s own conditions rather than assumed.

Which model fits depends on organizational scale, how mature existing risk functions already are, and how fast AI deployment is actually happening. A small organization deploying a handful of systems can centralize without becoming a bottleneck, while a large organization with dozens of business units deploying AI independently will choke a central function attempting to review everything itself.

A hybrid model is not a single fixed arrangement; it names the general strategy of splitting authority along some dimension other than “everything centralized” or “everything local,” and organizations choosing it still have to decide which dimension actually does the splitting. Some split by risk tier, centralizing review of the highest-risk systems while letting local teams self-certify lower-risk ones under a published standard. Some split by system class, holding predictive systems under an established credit-risk governance function while routing generative and agentic systems, newer and less matched to any existing process, through a dedicated review path. Section 13.3 develops one such split in detail, chosen for this chapter’s purposes because it tracks where defects actually originate, not because comparative evidence has established it as superior to every other split an organization might choose for its own conditions.

13.2 The operating model as a recurring variable

The operating model choice is not a single decision an organization makes once and records on an org chart. It recurs at every governance decision point independently, and an organization that has not noticed this will discover it has made the choice differently in different places without ever deciding to. Does the system inventory live centrally, or does each business unit maintain its own. Does a central function classify a system’s risk tier by one standard, or do local teams apply their own judgment. Does deployment go through a central gate, or local sign-off. Who holds model selection authority, and who owns the vendor relationship a purchased system requires.

An organization can be, entirely legitimately, federated on some of these and centralized on others, but the pattern needs to be a deliberate design, not an accident of which team happened to build which process first. Meridian’s own arrangement illustrates the accident version. The model risk management function had, over a decade, centralized model validation as a matter of course, while individual business lines had each built their own use-case approval process with no coordination between them, producing an organization centralized on one axis and federated on another. Neither arrangement was chosen on purpose.

13.3 One candidate split: federated at the system layer, centralized at the model layer

One candidate hybrid split, falling directly out of the two-record inventory Chapter 3 established, divides authority by layer rather than by function or business unit. Local teams own their own deployments. Those deployments are the use case, the affected population, the deployment context, and the day-to-day operation Chapter 8 through Chapter 10 describe. Model selection, developer terms, and provenance are held centrally. Held centrally means the question of which underlying models the organization has vetted, what obligations attach to each supply chain entry point Chapter 6 established, and what a model actually is before any local team decides to build a use case on top of it. Stated this way, the split can look cleaner than it actually is in practice. Several of the decisions it assigns are normally cross-layer processes, not exclusive territory, a point section 13.7’s governance-body discussion returns to concretely.

The reason this split is worth considering, not that it settles the allocation on its own, is that it tracks where a defect actually originates and where it needs to be caught once rather than rediscovered independently, at least for some decisions. A flaw in a purchased model’s training data or a vendor’s undisclosed model update is substantially a model-layer problem that will recur in every local deployment built on that model, and catching it centrally, once, can propagate the fix everywhere at once. A mismatch between a system’s intended use case and its actual deployment population is substantially a system-layer problem specific to one local team’s context, where a central function reviewing every local deployment decision has less comparative advantage than the team that understands its own population. But an inventory record, a risk classification, a deployment approval, and a vendor relationship each still need both local input and central coordination in practice. A central inventory needs local data plus central aggregation, and a vendor’s obligations can attach differently depending on customization, integration, data, and the specific regulatory role a given deployment plays. The layer split is therefore a starting frame for a decision-rights design, not the design itself.

The binary version of this split, one layer or the other holds each decision exclusively, undersells what the previous paragraph already flagged. Most of these decisions are cross-layer processes with a proposing party, an evidence-providing party, a challenging party, and an approving party, not a single owner. 13.4 How federated governance fails

Federated governance fails in predictable ways worth teaching directly, because a program that has not been warned about them tends to discover all three simultaneously. Classification can drift apart across units. Two business units facing comparable systems classify them at different risk tiers, and federated judgment, absent a shared calibration mechanism, can diverge over time rather than converge on its own. The drift itself is not automatically a defect. A hiring screen and a credit screen built on the same underlying model architecture can legitimately land in different risk tiers because their intended uses, affected populations, and consequences of error differ. A governance program that flags every difference as inconsistency would be punishing sound contextual judgment instead of catching a real failure. The failure worth catching is narrower. Two units facing the same use, the same population, and the same stakes can reach different classifications because the reviewers weighed identical evidence differently, with neither aware the other’s classification exists, and no recorded rationale either reviewer could compare against the other’s. Calibration is what tells the two apart. It means sampling classifications across units, comparing the recorded rationale and not only the tier assigned, and updating shared guidance where the comparison reveals unwarranted variation rather than a defensible contextual difference. Either way, the drift stays invisible until that comparison happens or an audit or incident forces it.

Local governance is captured by the business it serves. A reviewer embedded in the same business unit whose deployment they are reviewing faces continuous pressure to approve, since they share the unit’s incentives and its relationships, and independence erodes gradually, not through any single compromised decision. The reviewer does not experience this as compromise; they experience it as sound judgment calls made under time pressure by someone who understands the business, which is exactly why capture is hard to detect from inside the arrangement that produces it.

Nobody holds aggregate visibility, a particularly consequential failure because at least some regulatory reporting obligations attach to the deploying organization or a specific provider or deployer role under it, not to a business unit in isolation. Under the EU AI Act, Article 26 places monitoring, suspension, and notification duties on the deployer and Article 73 places serious-incident reporting duties on the provider, and either duty holder needs a view across whatever business units its own systems span to discharge it. A federated program with no central aggregation point cannot answer that kind of organization-or-role-wide question even when every individual business unit could answer its own piece of it correctly. An organization should be able to answer how many AI systems it operates and how that scope was defined. That is the standard Chapter 3’s inventory obligation sets for this book, and it is a standard that specific sector regulation or supervisory guidance can separately impose as its own legal recordkeeping duty. An organization has no answer at all if the inventory itself lives in a dozen disconnected spreadsheets rather than a single record, regardless of how well each business unit tracks its own piece of it.

13.5 Roles and responsibilities

A governance decision needs an explicit answer to who is responsible for doing the work, who is accountable for the outcome, who must be consulted before the decision is made, and who merely needs to be informed after it is. This is the RACI structure, borrowed from broader risk practice and applied here to AI-specific decisions instead of left as a generic template. A deployment decision under Chapter 8’s readiness dimensions might assign the deploying business unit as responsible for assembling the readiness evidence and accountable for the underlying use decision. It might assign a central AI governance function as holding required concurrence, an approval a deployment cannot proceed without. The model risk function and legal would then be consulted before the decision, and the executive sponsor would be informed once it is made. Required concurrence is a second-line control right, not a claim that AI governance owns the deployment decision itself; section 13.6 develops why keeping that distinction sharp matters for the independence a second-line function needs to actually challenge a first-line decision. Written this specifically, a RACI assignment resolves what a vaguer “governance reviews deployments” leaves open. It states how deployments get reviewed, with what authority to actually block one, and who is left holding responsibility when a deployment goes wrong.

An incident response RACI, drawn from Chapter 10’s ten-stage lifecycle, follows the same logic with different names filling the roles, but it needs one correction the deployment-decision RACI does not. Legal and privacy review cannot sit as a sequential gate before notification the way “consulted before the decision” works for a deployment. Chapter 10’s regulatory clocks, Article 73’s provider outer limits and Article 26’s deployer duties, are not yet in force for most systems. Annex III’s high-risk obligations take effect 2 December 2027, checked against the consolidated EU AI Act as of 2026-09-12, and only then do both clocks start running from the moment of awareness rather than from the moment an internal consultation finishes. Different incidents also route to different legal actors and jurisdictions depending on the organization’s role and the incident type. The team operating the system is responsible for the containment step, since they are the ones positioned to halt it or, for an agentic system, to trigger the circuit breaker Chapter 12 described. The AI governance function is accountable for confirming the provisional severity classification and signing off on the post-incident review’s owned, dated remediation. Legal and, where personal data is involved, privacy work in parallel with containment and investigation, not ahead of them. They identify the applicable legal role and jurisdiction and prepare whatever notification the clock requires, including an initial report that is incomplete where the rules allow it, rather than waiting for a complete investigation to finish first. The executive sponsor and, for the most severe tier, the board are informed once containment is underway instead of waiting for the investigation to close. The same four-role structure applies to a deployment decision and to an incident response. But an organization applying it needs to recognize where the incident-response version’s timing constraints differ from the deployment version’s, precisely because RACI states who does what and does not, by itself, state when.

13.6 The three lines model

The three lines model, familiar from risk management generally, needs full explanation in AI-specific terms, not a passing reference assuming the reader already knows how it maps onto this book’s subject. This section follows the IIA’s current statement of the model, updated 8 July 2026 and checked against that update on 2026-09-12, which describes roles rather than department names. The IIA is explicit that “lines” name complementary roles and relationships operating concurrently, not a rank order, an org chart, or a sequence of checkpoints one decision passes through in turn. The rest of this section keeps that framing even where a specific example, like the deployment sequence in section 13.5, describes roles acting at different points in time. The first line owns and manages risk directly. It is the teams building, selecting, and deploying AI systems, who make the day-to-day decisions this book’s earlier chapters describe and who bear primary accountability for those decisions being sound. The second line provides oversight, standards, and challenge without taking over the first line’s underlying decision. An AI governance function occupies this line, setting standards, reviewing deployments against them, holding a required-concurrence right where section 13.5 assigned one, and maintaining the aggregate visibility section 13.4 showed a federated program can otherwise lose. It often does this alongside a model risk management function handling the technical validation piece specifically. Holding a concurrence right that can stop a deployment is a second-line control, not evidence that the function has moved to the first line. The two remain distinguishable by who owns the underlying business decision versus who can block it on risk grounds. A governance program that treats “has veto power” as a signal of first-line ownership has lost the independence the second line’s challenge role depends on. The third line provides independent assurance to the board and senior leadership that the first two lines are actually functioning as designed. That assurance comes from internal audit, testing the governance program itself rather than any individual AI system, on a mandate independent of both the teams deploying systems and the function governing them. A model risk management function and an AI governance function sitting side by side are not automatically “the two lines” in the sense this section describes. Whether they occupy the same line or different lines depends on each one’s actual role in a given decision, which needs stating explicitly instead of being assumed from their names.

Agentic systems increase the frequency, speed, and scope of this pressure instead of inventing it from nothing. Predictive systems already require the ongoing first-line monitoring and outcomes analysis current model risk management guidance calls for, since drift, misuse, and process overrides can all surface after deployment and none of them wait for a scheduled review. What changes with an agentic system is that Chapter 12’s permission scope and runtime intervention have to be designed before the agent acts rather than reviewed after a single output. A first line that owns day-to-day operation of an agent is, functionally, exercising a risk decision every time the agent takes an action within its granted scope, at a frequency a predictive system’s periodic monitoring cycle does not approach. The second line’s job shifts correspondingly, from periodic deployment review toward standards for what permission scopes are acceptable and audit of the action logs Chapter 12 required. A governance program that has not adjusted its second-line practice for this shift will find itself reviewing agentic deployments with the same pre-deployment checklist built for systems that only ever produced a discrete output to review.

Chapters 11, 12, and this chapter name controls along different axes, and the difference matters for tracing a specific control back to a specific owner rather than losing the connection between a technical safeguard and the role accountable for it. Chapter 11 classifies a control by its effect, reducing likelihood, detecting, containing before release, correcting, or transferring residual risk. Chapter 12 classifies a control by where it sits in the pipeline, inline enforcement, near-real-time detection, aggregate monitoring, or retrospective audit. An inline enforcement point, for instance, can carry a preventive effect, a detective one, or both, depending on what it is configured to do. What stays constant across both axes, and across this chapter’s own vocabulary of prevent, detect, contain, intervene, recover, verify, and learn, is the underlying obligation regardless of which axis names the control. Each one still needs a first-line owner who runs it, a second-line function that sets its standard and challenges it, and, where the control failed and the failure was material, a third-line assurance check that the standard itself is working. A registry record, a permission scope, or a claim-verification step this book described in Chapters 11 and 12 without naming a role is, concretely, one of the decisions this chapter’s RACI and three-lines structure has to assign. Leaving it unassigned is not a gap this chapter’s structure tolerates. It is exactly the gap this chapter exists to close.

13.7 Governance bodies

A governance body, a committee convened to review or approve AI systems, needs more than composition, mandate, and decision rights stated to actually function as a governance mechanism, not a discussion group, though those three are the necessary starting point. Composition names who sits on it and why, ensuring the perspectives a decision actually requires, technical, legal, business, and affected-party representation where relevant, are present rather than assembled from whoever was available. Mandate states what the body is actually empowered to decide, distinct from what it is merely invited to discuss. Decision rights specify whether the body’s judgment is binding, advisory, or escalates to someone else entirely. A committee that spends an hour debating a deployment and then watches the business unit proceed regardless has decision rights of zero, regardless of how substantive its discussion was.

Naming these three does not yet make a body operable on an ordinary Tuesday morning. A working governance body also needs intake criteria for what reaches it and what is handled below it, a quorum requirement, a conflict-of-interest check for members with a stake in the specific decision, and an evidence pack a submitter must assemble before the meeting. It needs a stated decision standard, minutes that record dissent as well as the outcome, a conditional-approval option with a stated exception duration, and an escalation path when the body cannot reach a decision. It also needs an appeal route for a submitter who disagrees with the outcome, action tracking for anything the approval was conditioned on, and a re-review trigger for a material change to the approved system. Finally, it needs an emergency route for a decision that cannot wait for the body’s normal cadence, and periodic review of whether the body’s own decisions are holding up in practice. A committee with a charter naming its composition, mandate, and decision rights but none of this operating detail still functions ceremonially, approving whatever evidence happens to be presented to it instead of actually governing the decision.

A documented illustration of a governance body being stood up at close to this level of structural specificity, though not to every element listed above, comes from outside banking entirely. The White House Office of Management and Budget’s Memorandum M-25-21, issued 3 April 2025 and rescinding the prior administration’s M-24-10, requires the head of each U.S. federal agency to retain or designate a Chief AI Officer within sixty days. It separately requires each CFO Act agency, not every federal agency, to convene an AI Governance Board within ninety days, while permitting an existing governance body to serve that function, and it specifies the board’s chair, vice-chair, and required functional representation. Alongside these, a cross-agency Chief AI Officer Council, convened by OMB itself, coordinates AI governance across the federal government as a whole (checked 2026-09-12). The memorandum states a requirement; it does not prove every covered agency met it. The U.S. International Trade Commission’s own AI page reports that it re-designated its Chief AI Officer and retained its AI Governance Board, naming the board’s thirteen member positions, which is direct evidence of the requirement actually implemented rather than only imposed (checked 2026-09-13). Neither source specifies the quorum, evidence-pack, or dissent-recording detail this section argues a body needs to actually function, which is precisely the gap between naming a governance body and operating one that this section is making.

13.8 Acceptable use and sanctioned tooling

Unapproved AI use, employees using AI tools the organization has not reviewed or approved, often because a sanctioned alternative is slower or less capable where one exists at all, is the practical problem an acceptable use policy exists to address. Current government guidance on this exact pattern, published 7 September 2026, recommends understanding what need drove the unapproved use, building a positive security culture, offering an approved alternative, and reducing risk directly, instead of treating a written prohibition as sufficient on its own. A prohibition may be genuinely necessary for particular data, tools, or uses, and this section does not claim every prohibition backfires. What it does claim, consistent with that guidance, is that policy text alone rarely creates the visibility or the safe alternative an organization needs, so a prohibition unaccompanied by anything else risks losing visibility into use it cannot detect rather than eliminating the use itself. A fuller acceptable use policy combines clear use rules with an approved tool that meets legitimate needs where one can be provided, proportionate access and data-loss controls, and procurement and security review of new tools before they spread informally. It also combines awareness training and a reporting channel for tools employees want but do not have, monitoring consistent with law and internal policy, and a defined response path for discovered unapproved use that does not default to discipline alone. Finally, it combines periodic measurement of how much unapproved use remains, so the policy’s effect can actually be assessed instead of assumed.

Combining all of this into a policy still leaves an individual employee needing one place that states these things concretely. That place should state which tools and accounts are approved; which tasks and data are permitted, restricted, or prohibited by classification; where human review of AI output is required before anyone relies on it; and how output accuracy gets verified. It should also state how intellectual property, confidentiality, and privacy obligations apply to AI-assisted work; what security requirements attach to an approved tool; what records the employee must keep; and what disclosure the organization owes a counterparty or regulator about AI-assisted output. And it should state how to report an incident or a tool the policy does not cover; how an exception gets requested and granted; what happens when the policy is violated; and how often the policy gets checked against the tools employees are asking for.

13.9 Conflict with incumbent functions and executive engagement

Where a model risk management function already exists with real authority, as it did at Meridian for a decade before FAIRLEND’s AI governance function was chartered, a new AI governance function arrives as a structural competitor, not a blank slate. Pretending otherwise produces exactly the kind of unresolved friction the case in focus below works through. But the boundary a new function should draw is not the one an assumption of empty incumbent territory would suggest. Current U.S. federal banking supervisory guidance on model risk management, issued April 2026 and superseding the prior 2011 guidance, directly addresses model purpose, intended and actual use, limitations, misuse, validation, monitoring, aggregate exposure, and third-party models. So an incumbent function governed by that guidance already has a real, if partial, mandate over questions of use appropriateness, not only technical accuracy. A new AI governance function that assumes otherwise is working from an outdated picture of what model risk management is actually built to check. This specific conflict, and the supervisory guidance resolving its boundaries, is banking-specific. A nonfinancial organization facing the same structural pattern, a new AI governance function meeting an established data governance board or cybersecurity risk committee with its own reporting line and its own settled scope, runs the identical joint-responsibility analysis this section develops. It does this without a comparable federal supervisory framework already dictating what the incumbent function’s mandate covers. This is exactly why the analysis itself, not the specific banking citations, is the part that transfers. That guidance does not cover fundamental-rights and disclosure obligations, contestability, affected-party requirements broader than model performance, and governance of the system classes outside the guidance’s stated coverage. It leaves those gaps because it addresses model risk specifically rather than every AI-specific legal or social-impact question, and because it explicitly excludes generative and agentic models from its own scope. The correct starting point is therefore a joint-responsibility analysis. It maps what the incumbent mandate already covers, identifies the AI-specific duties it does not, and assigns the decisions that fall in both territories explicitly. This is different from a new function asserting or conceding a three-question territory that neither the incumbent’s actual mandate nor its actual gaps line up with.

Executive and board engagement closes the loop this chapter has built. A board needs regular reporting on the aggregate picture section 13.4 showed federated governance can lose, the specific questions to ask of any significant deployment, and whether the readiness dimensions Chapter 8 requires were actually evaluated. It also needs to know whether an incident this year exposed a systemic gap rather than an isolated failure. What a board’s oversight duty actually requires depends on the entity’s jurisdiction and legal form, a question this book does not resolve in general. But reporting built to answer these questions is what lets a board exercise whatever oversight duty applies to it, rather than receiving reassurance dressed as reporting regardless of which duty governs.

The cadence matters as much as the content, though no single cadence is the correct one for every board. A board relying on annual reporting alone, with no operational monitoring or event-driven escalation underneath it, can learn about a systemic gap long after it opened rather than while it is still forming. That lag is too slow for a function whose own inventory Chapter 3 requires to be current continuously. Paired with the continuous monitoring and escalation triggers described below, an annual reporting cycle does not carry the same lag. So the problem this chapter warns against is the missing pairing, not the annual cadence by itself. Quarterly reporting is one example of a cadence some boards already use for other risk reporting. Adopting a similar rhythm for AI governance can let a function surface a drifting classification pattern, a rising rate of near-miss incidents, or a business unit consistently pushing deployment decisions past the readiness bar. It can do this while the pattern is still a trend, not an already-realized failure. Whatever the routine cadence, it needs to be paired with event-driven escalation for defined triggers, a serious incident, a threshold breach, a material change, that reach the board or the relevant committee immediately rather than waiting for the next scheduled report. A board relying on a fixed cadence alone learns about an urgent problem only as fast as its calendar allows. The report itself should name what changed since the last one instead of restating the program’s structure every cycle, because a board that reads the same description of the operating model repeatedly has learned nothing new about whether the model is actually working.

Case in focus: a mandate that had to be negotiated, not assumed

FAIRLEND · Hypothetical, following the running cases

This continuing fictional scenario illustrates the conflict pattern section 13.9 describes; it does not report an event that actually occurred at any specific organization. Meridian’s board chartered its AI governance function with a mandate spanning fairness, transparency, and risk review across every AI system in the organization, a document that read, on its face, as comprehensive. The model risk management function, governing FAIRLEND’s credit models under a supervisory framework it had operated under for years, read the same charter as an encroachment on territory it had held without dispute since before “AI governance” was a phrase anyone at Meridian used. The first several deployment reviews under the new charter produced the conflict pattern section 13.9 describes. Both functions showed up to review the same model, each unclear on which one’s judgment actually controlled the outcome, and each carrying a different picture of what the other’s mandate actually already covered.

The resolution followed a joint-responsibility analysis, not either function conceding or asserting exclusive territory. Meridian first mapped what its model risk management function’s existing mandate, governed by current supervisory guidance, already covered for FAIRLEND. That mandate covered not only technical validation but model purpose, intended use, and monitoring for misuse, a broader mandate than the AI governance function had initially assumed. AI governance then identified the specific gaps that mandate did not close. Those gaps were whether the population a proposed lending-product extension would newly reach matched the population FAIRLEND‘s fairness testing had actually covered, and whether Meridian could satisfy the adverse-action explanation duties Chapter 2 introduces and produce the decision-level evidence Chapter 15 develops. Chapter 8 makes both matters conditions of deployment readiness, not the source of the underlying legal duty. Both questions required AI-specific and legal expertise the model-risk mandate did not claim to provide. Instead of assigning these as separate, walled-off territories, Meridian required both functions’ sign-off on any deployment touching either question, with model risk management’s validation evidence feeding directly into the readiness record AI governance’s concurrence decision required.

Working out which of the model risk management function and the AI governance function occupied which line of the three lines model, rather than assuming the two were automatically “the two lines” simply because two functions existed, was itself part of the resolution. Both turned out to sit in the second line for FAIRLEND’s deployment decisions, one providing technical challenge and one providing AI-specific and fairness challenge, with the deploying business unit remaining the first-line owner of the underlying decision in both cases.

The stalled product launch that forced this mapping into being carried a cost the board tracked directly. That cost included a delayed launch, a business unit sponsor who had learned to route around both functions on the next deployment rather than repeat the experience, and a boundary that existed on paper because a deployment had already stalled to force it into being. The lesson Meridian drew, and the one this chapter draws from it, is that the joint-responsibility mapping should happen before the first contested deployment, not during it. A boundary drawn under the pressure of a stalled launch is drawn worse than one drawn in advance, with more resentment on both sides and less attention paid to whether the mapping actually tracks what each function’s mandate does and does not already cover.

Summary

Centralized, federated, and hybrid operating models are design patterns rather than a performance ranking. The right choice depends on organizational scale, existing risk maturity, deployment velocity, risk concentration, and independence requirements, tested against an organization’s own conditions, not assumed from any one pattern’s general reputation. The operating model choice recurs at every governance decision point instead of being made once on an org chart, and an organization that has not noticed this is often centralized on one axis and federated on another by accident, not by design. One candidate split divides authority by layer, federated at the system layer where local context matters most, centralized at the model layer where a defect needs to be caught once, not rediscovered independently. Most individual decisions this split touches, though, are cross-layer processes needing a decision-rights matrix instead of a two-column allocation.

Federated governance fails through classification drift, capture by the business a reviewer serves, and lost aggregate visibility. This is a particularly consequential failure because at least some regulatory reporting obligations attach to the organization or a specific provider or deployer role rather than to a single business unit. RACI applied to governance decisions makes responsibility, accountability, consultation, and information explicit instead of assumed, though an incident-response RACI needs its consultation step run in parallel with containment and investigation, not as a sequential gate ahead of a regulatory notification clock. The three lines model, fully mapped onto AI-specific functions per the IIA’s current statement, separates first-line ownership, second-line oversight and challenge, including a required-concurrence right that does not by itself make the second line an owner, and third-line independent assurance. A governance body needs composition, mandate, and decision rights stated explicitly, plus the operating detail, intake criteria, quorum, evidence pack, dissent record, escalation, and re-review triggers, that actually makes it function instead of merely existing. An acceptable use policy addresses unapproved AI use only by combining a sanctioned alternative where one can be offered with access controls, monitoring, training, and measurement, not a written prohibition alone.

Conflict with an incumbent function like model risk management is structural rather than avoidable, and resolving it requires a joint-responsibility analysis, not an assumed empty territory. An incumbent function governed by current supervisory guidance may already have real authority over use appropriateness and not only technical validation. The productive first step, then, is mapping what its mandate already covers, identifying the AI-specific duties it does not, and assigning the overlap explicitly instead of either function conceding or asserting exclusive ground.
